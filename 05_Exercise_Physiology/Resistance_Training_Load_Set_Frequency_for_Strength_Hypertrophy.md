# Resistance Training Load / Set / Frequency for Strength and Hypertrophy

## One-Sentence Definition

Resistance training load / set / frequency prescription is a practical way to code RTx dose by intensity, per-exercise volume, and weekly exposure when comparing strength and hypertrophy adaptations in healthy adults.

## Definition and Boundary

This page covers one concept: how load, sets, and weekly frequency structure supervised resistance training prescription for strength and hypertrophy in healthy adults.

It does not cover:

- resistance training safety in chronic disease or inpatient rehabilitation
- special-population progression rules
- sports performance periodization
- home-based unsupervised RT
- complete proximity-to-failure prescription
- nutritional support for hypertrophy

Currier et al. 2023 coded prescriptions as `XY#`:

- `H`: higher load, at least 80% 1RM.
- `L`: lower load, below 80% 1RM.
- `M`: multiset.
- `S`: single set.
- `1`, `2`, `3`: once weekly, twice weekly, or at least three days/week.

Example: `HM2` means higher-load, multiset, twice-weekly resistance training.

## Why It Matters

Exercise prescription often names only the exercise type, but RT adaptation depends on the dose package. Load, number of sets, and weekly frequency jointly determine the training stimulus and the realistic adherence burden.

For healthy adults, the main practical point is not "only one RTx works." The better reading is:

- any feasible RT is generally better than no RT for strength and hypertrophy
- higher load becomes more relevant when optimizing strength
- multiple sets become more relevant when optimizing hypertrophy
- preference and feasibility can justify non-top-ranked RTx

## Preconditions or Conditions

The Currier 2023 inference applies best when these conditions are met:

- adult aged at least 18 years
- generally healthy and community-dwelling
- supervised resistance training
- intervention duration at least 6 weeks
- outcome is muscle strength or muscle size
- RTx can be categorized into the load / set / frequency nodes used in the study

It should not be directly applied to:

- elite athletes or military personnel
- people with cardiovascular disease, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, cancer, peripheral artery disease, osteoarthritis, or other comorbidities
- acute injury or post-surgical repair
- inpatient, outpatient rehabilitation, or long-term care settings
- explicitly obese or overweight study populations
- frail older adults
- unsupervised home-based RT

## Mechanism

### Stepwise Derivation

1. Strength outcome depends partly on the ability to express high force in a tested movement.
2. Higher-load RT is more specific to high-force testing demands, especially 1RM-like outcomes.
3. Hypertrophy depends on cumulative muscle stimulus across repeated contractions.
4. Multiple sets increase accumulated work and likely total hypertrophic stimulus.
5. Weekly frequency distributes exposure and may raise total volume or adherence opportunity.
6. A prescription node such as `HM2` or `LM2` is therefore not one variable; it is a package of load, set structure, and weekly exposure.
7. Network meta-analysis compares these packages using direct and indirect randomized evidence.

## Observable Patterns

- Strength: higher-load multiset prescriptions ranked highest; `HM3`, `HM2`, and `HM1` had the highest top-three probabilities.
- Hypertrophy: multiple-set prescriptions ranked highest; `HM2`, `LM1`, and `LM2` had the highest top-three probabilities.
- Compared with no exercise, most RTx improved strength and hypertrophy.
- Between RT prescriptions, most pairwise credible intervals included zero, so exact hierarchy should be read cautiously.
- Sparse nodes can look less effective because estimates are imprecise, not necessarily because the prescription is biologically ineffective.

## Clinical / Research Implication

### For Healthy Adults

- If the goal is strength optimization, use higher load when safe, tolerated, and technically adequate.
- If the goal is hypertrophy, do not reduce the prescription to load alone; sets / volume matter.
- If adherence is the bottleneck, a lower-load or less frequent RTx may still be clinically useful because doing some RT is better than doing none.
- Do not discourage a feasible non-top-ranked RTx when the alternative is no RT.

### For Rehabilitation

- This page can inform the exercise physiology background, but it is not a disease-specific therapeutic exercise rule.
- Clinical RT prescription still needs screening, contraindications, symptom monitoring, technique observation, progression criteria, and referral criteria.
- For special populations, connect this framework to [[../02_方法學/Repetitions_in_Reserve_RIR_阻力訓練強度處方]] and disease-specific pages rather than importing healthy-adult rankings directly.

## Fact

- Currier et al. 2023 included randomized trials of healthy adults aged 18 years or older.
- The analysis excluded athletes, military personnel, people with comorbidities, injured persons, rehabilitation patients, long-term care residents, unsupervised RT, and interventions shorter than 6 weeks.
- Strength NMA included 178 studies and 5097 participants.
- Hypertrophy NMA included 119 studies and 3364 participants.
- Compared with CTRL, the largest strength estimate was `HM3`.
- Compared with CTRL, the largest hypertrophy estimate was `HM2`.
- Most between-RTx comparisons had 95% credible intervals that included zero.
- Network meta-regression did not show obvious modifying effects from age, training status, proportion female, duration, volitional fatigue, relative weekly volume load, measurement tool / region, or publication year.
- The source states that effects of different RTx on health outcomes remain largely unknown.

## Inference

- Higher-load RT is a reasonable default direction when the explicit goal is maximal strength gain in healthy adults.
- Multiple sets are a reasonable default direction when the explicit goal is hypertrophy in healthy adults.
- Lower-load RT can still be a legitimate strength intervention compared with no exercise, especially when high load is not preferred or not feasible.
- The best clinical prescription is not necessarily the top-ranked NMA node; it is the highest-value feasible node for the person and context.

## Assumption

- The `80% 1RM` split meaningfully separates higher-load from lower-load prescriptions.
- Study outcomes measured by different tools can be standardized enough for NMA comparison.
- Direct and indirect randomized comparisons are sufficiently transitive for broad ranking.
- Group-level results can guide general healthy-adult prescription while still requiring individual adjustment.

## Uncertainty

- Exact top-ranked nodes may change with sparse evidence, bias, and coding choices.
- The dose-response curve for load, sets, and frequency was not modeled continuously.
- Periodized RT programs and overlapping load ranges were not represented.
- Rest interval, tempo, repetition velocity, time under tension, and proximity to failure were not fully integrated.
- Physical function outcomes had limited evidence.
- Health outcome effects of different RTx remain uncertain.

## Limitations and Misreadings

- Misreading: `HM2` or `HM3` is the only correct prescription.
  - Correction: these are top-ranked nodes within one healthy-adult NMA framework, not universal rules.
- Misreading: hypertrophy always requires high load.
  - Correction: multiple-set prescriptions were more salient for hypertrophy ranking; load alone did not dominate.
- Misreading: training to failure is required.
  - Correction: volitional fatigue did not improve model fit in this NMA; trained individuals may still require separate evidence.
- Misreading: this applies to rehabilitation patients.
  - Correction: rehabilitation, chronic disease, injury, frailty, and several other populations were excluded.
- Misreading: no difference between RTx means all prescriptions are equivalent.
  - Correction: wide credible intervals and sparse nodes limit the ability to prove equivalence.
