# Source Summary: Long-term antithrombotic therapy for the secondary prevention of ischemic stroke

## One-Sentence Summary

For long-term secondary prevention after noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or TIA, the default is single antiplatelet therapy with aspirin, clopidogrel, or aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole; long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel is generally avoided, while anticoagulation or combination antiplatelet-anticoagulant therapy belongs only to specific mechanisms or selected high-risk atherosclerotic scenarios.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Long-term antiplatelet default in noncardioembolic ischemic stroke / TIA

#### One-Sentence Definition

Long-term antithrombotic therapy for noncardioembolic ischemic stroke / TIA is primarily single antiplatelet therapy, selected by stroke mechanism, tolerance, contraindications, cost, access, and special population factors.

#### Known Facts

- The source reviews antiplatelet therapy for secondary stroke prevention and separates this from acute antithrombotic therapy.
- For atherothrombotic, lacunar, or cryptogenic noncardioembolic ischemic stroke / TIA, the source recommends antiplatelet therapy with aspirin, clopidogrel, or aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole.
- The source suggests clopidogrel 75 mg daily monotherapy or aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole 25/200 mg twice daily rather than aspirin alone when tolerated and accessible.
- Aspirin remains appropriate when clopidogrel or aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole is not tolerated, contraindicated, unaffordable, or unavailable.
- When aspirin is used for secondary prevention, the source recommends 50 to 100 mg daily; lower doses up to 325 mg daily have fewer gastrointestinal and bleeding complications than higher doses.
- Cilostazol is reasonable for patients of East Asian ethnicity and may be used when standard agents are unavailable or not tolerated; evidence in non-East Asian populations is very limited.

#### Mechanism Chain

Ischemic stroke / TIA recurrence risk persists after the acute event -> noncardioembolic mechanisms often involve platelet-rich arterial thrombosis or atherothrombosis -> antiplatelet therapy reduces platelet activation/aggregation -> fewer arterial thrombotic events -> reduced recurrent ischemic stroke and other vascular events.

#### Inferences

- If the stroke mechanism has not been clarified, choosing an antithrombotic agent is premature; the mechanism determines whether antiplatelet, anticoagulation, revascularization, or another intervention is appropriate.
- For rehabilitation or outpatient follow-up, medication reconciliation should not merely ask whether the patient is "on blood thinner"; it should ask whether the agent matches the documented stroke mechanism.

#### Assumptions

- The patient has no active bleeding, prohibitive bleeding risk, severe medication allergy, or other contraindication.
- The ischemic stroke / TIA mechanism has been sufficiently evaluated to classify it as noncardioembolic when applying the antiplatelet framework.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Optimal response to apparent breakthrough stroke despite antiplatelet adherence is uncertain.
- Routine platelet function or genotype testing for aspirin or clopidogrel resistance is not supported by proven outcome benefit in this context.
- Cilostazol generalizability outside East Asian populations is not well established.

### Concept: Limited role of long-term dual antiplatelet therapy

#### One-Sentence Definition

Long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel is not the default secondary prevention strategy for most noncardioembolic stroke / TIA patients because bleeding risk increases without sufficient added efficacy.

#### Known Facts

- Short-term DAPT after acute ischemic stroke or TIA is a separate acute-phase topic.
- For most patients with noncardioembolic stroke or TIA, the source recommends against long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel.
- Exceptions are driven by other indications, such as recent acute coronary syndrome, arterial stent placement, or carotid stenting, and even then DAPT is usually time-limited.
- Carotid stenting is commonly followed by aspirin plus clopidogrel for 30 days, then long-term single-agent antiplatelet therapy.
- Carotid endarterectomy is usually managed with aspirin 81 to 325 mg daily started before surgery and continued indefinitely unless a long-term anticoagulation indication exists.

#### Mechanism Chain

Two antiplatelet pathways blocked simultaneously -> stronger inhibition of platelet aggregation -> possible early ischemic benefit in selected acute settings -> accumulated long-term bleeding risk -> loss of net benefit when used indefinitely for most noncardioembolic stroke / TIA patients.

#### Inferences

- A patient discharged on DAPT after minor stroke or high-risk TIA requires a stop date and a planned transition to single antiplatelet therapy.
- Indefinite DAPT should trigger chart review for a non-stroke indication, such as coronary stent, ACS, or carotid stent.

#### Assumptions

- The patient is not within an acute short-term DAPT window and does not have an independent cardiac or vascular stent indication.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Some special populations may need individualized neurology-cardiology decisions, especially patients with concurrent coronary artery disease or recent stenting.

### Concept: Anticoagulation and combination therapy boundaries

#### One-Sentence Definition

Anticoagulation is reserved for mechanisms that require it, while aspirin plus low-dose rivaroxaban is a selected option for some nonlacunar noncardioembolic patients with systemic atherosclerosis rather than a universal substitute for antiplatelet therapy.

#### Known Facts

- Atrial fibrillation-associated recurrent stroke prevention is treated primarily with anticoagulation and is discussed elsewhere by the source.
- Patients with ischemic stroke / TIA due to atrial fibrillation or another anticoagulation indication are generally not treated with concomitant antiplatelet therapy because bleeding risk rises.
- Left ventricular thrombus is treated with anticoagulation; timing after acute stroke depends on hemorrhagic conversion risk.
- Cryptogenic stroke, including ESUS, is generally not itself an indication for anticoagulation; antiplatelet therapy is preferred in most such cases.
- Aspirin plus rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily is described as reasonable for some high-risk patients with noncardioembolic, nonlacunar stroke and evidence of peripheral, coronary, or cerebrovascular atherosclerosis.
- Factor XIa inhibition is presented as an emerging possible future class; it should not be treated as established routine care from this source alone.

#### Mechanism Chain

Cardioembolic clot formation or LV thrombus -> coagulation-driven thrombus -> anticoagulation targets thrombin/factor Xa or vitamin K-dependent clotting pathways.  
Noncardioembolic atherothrombosis -> platelet activation predominates -> antiplatelet therapy is default.  
High-risk systemic atherosclerosis -> both platelet and low-grade coagulation pathways may contribute -> selected low-dose anticoagulant plus aspirin may reduce ischemic events but increases bleeding risk.

#### Inferences

- "Cryptogenic" should not be used as a shortcut to anticoagulation; it should prompt extended evaluation for occult atrial fibrillation, structural cardiac source, paradoxical embolism, vasculopathy, or hypercoagulable state.
- Combination antiplatelet-anticoagulant therapy needs explicit indication and bleeding-risk review.

#### Assumptions

- The clinician can distinguish lacunar stroke, nonlacunar atherosclerotic stroke, cardioembolic stroke, and ESUS with adequate diagnostic workup.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- The best way to identify which prior-stroke patients benefit from aspirin plus low-dose rivaroxaban remains individualized.
- Factor XIa inhibitors require guideline and regulatory updates before being treated as standard therapy.

### Concept: Antiplatelet treatment failure

#### One-Sentence Definition

Breakthrough ischemic events on antiplatelet therapy should trigger reassessment of adherence, stroke mechanism, and modifiable risk factors rather than reflex routine resistance testing.

#### Known Facts

- Antiplatelet treatment failure means thrombotic or ischemic event despite adherence to recommended-dose antiplatelet therapy.
- Possible explanations include nonadherence, antiplatelet nonresponsiveness, incorrect stroke mechanism, uncontrolled risk factors, and nonmodifiable risk factors.
- Nonadherence is believed to be the most frequent cause of apparent antiplatelet resistance.
- There is no proven role for routine aspirin or clopidogrel resistance testing by laboratory or genetic testing in this setting.
- For breakthrough stroke while on aspirin, the benefit of switching to another antithrombotic regimen remains unproven, although the source generally suggests changing to a different antiplatelet if nonadherence is not evident.

#### Mechanism Chain

Recurrent ischemic event despite therapy -> possibilities include no drug exposure, poor biologic response, wrong target mechanism, or persistent risk drivers -> mechanism reassessment and adherence review determine whether changing antiplatelet therapy is rational.

#### Inferences

- CYP2C19 genotype can matter pharmacologically, but this source does not support routine testing as a default stroke-secondary-prevention workflow.
- A "failed aspirin" label can be misleading if the recurrent event was due to occult atrial fibrillation, LV thrombus, cancer-associated hypercoagulability, or hemodynamic insufficiency.

#### Assumptions

- The recurrent event is confirmed ischemic stroke, TIA, or vascular occlusive event rather than a mimic.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Optimal antithrombotic strategy after true breakthrough noncardioembolic stroke remains uncertain.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Long-term noncardioembolic stroke / TIA: use a single antiplatelet agent unless there is a time-limited DAPT indication or another mechanism-specific indication.
- Acceptable first-line long-term antiplatelet choices: aspirin, clopidogrel, aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole.
- UpToDate preference when feasible: clopidogrel monotherapy or aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole rather than aspirin alone, but aspirin remains appropriate when access, tolerance, or contraindications determine choice.
- Avoid long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel as routine secondary prevention.
- Prasugrel is generally avoided in patients with prior stroke/TIA because of bleeding risk.
- For carotid stenting, DAPT is usually 30 days, then single antiplatelet therapy.
- For carotid endarterectomy, aspirin monotherapy is generally continued indefinitely unless anticoagulation is separately indicated.
- For AF or LV thrombus, manage as anticoagulation-indicated stroke mechanisms rather than as simple antiplatelet failure.
- Cryptogenic stroke / ESUS does not automatically mean anticoagulation.
- In patients age 75 or older or otherwise at increased gastrointestinal bleeding risk, proton pump inhibitor coadministration is reasonable.

## Research-Useful Points

- Factor XIa inhibition is an emerging class with potential relevance to noncardioembolic ischemic stroke secondary prevention, but this remains a future-update area.
- Cilostazol evidence is regionally concentrated in East Asian populations; generalizability and implementation outside those groups remain unresolved.
- Breakthrough stroke on antiplatelet therapy remains an area where mechanism reassessment is clearer than the evidence for any single switch strategy.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with: "DAPT helped early after minor stroke/TIA, so it should continue indefinitely." The source separates short-term acute DAPT from long-term secondary prevention and advises against routine long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel.
- Conflicts with: "ESUS should be anticoagulated because it looks embolic." The source states cryptogenic stroke / ESUS is generally not an anticoagulation indication.
- Conflicts with: "CYP2C19 or platelet function testing should be routine after stroke." The source does not support routine resistance testing as a proven outcome-improving strategy.
- Conflicts with: "Any stroke patient with atherosclerosis should receive aspirin plus rivaroxaban." The source limits this to selected high-risk nonlacunar, noncardioembolic patients with systemic atherosclerosis and requires bleeding-risk consideration.
