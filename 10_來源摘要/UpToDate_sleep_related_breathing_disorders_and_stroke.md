## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: OSA as Stroke Risk Factor

#### One-Sentence Definition

OSA as stroke risk factor 是指 obstructive sleep apnea 可透過 intermittent hypoxia、sympathetic activation、endothelial dysfunction、blood pressure / AF / prothrombotic effects and impaired cerebral hemodynamic reserve 增加 ischemic stroke risk 的機制框架。

#### Known Facts

- OSA has been associated with increased ischemic stroke risk independent of vascular risk factors.
- Other sleep-related breathing disorders, including CSA syndrome, are less well studied as stroke risk factors.
- Prospective cohort evidence suggests dose-response relation between OSA severity and stroke risk.
- A large male cohort found highest obstructive AHI quartile associated with ischemic stroke risk after adjustment.
- In a cohort of women with possible OSA, untreated OSA was associated with increased incident stroke risk, while treated OSA was not.
- CPAP trials for primary cardiovascular event reduction have reported no clear difference in stroke or cardiovascular events versus controls, possibly affected by insufficient treatment adherence or intensity.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
OSA
-> recurrent upper airway obstruction and negative intrathoracic pressure
-> intermittent hypoxia, sympathetic activation, endothelial dysfunction and impaired CO2 vasomotor response
-> blood pressure / AF / diabetes / platelet / coagulation / inflammation effects
-> lower cerebral hemodynamic reserve or embolic risk
-> increased stroke susceptibility, especially with vascular stenosis or poor reserve
```

#### Inferences

- OSA should be treated as a modifiable vascular risk signal, not merely a sleep quality complaint.
- OSA may matter most when it interacts with hypertension, AF, intracranial stenosis, PFO or impaired hemodynamic reserve.

#### Assumptions

- The patient population is adult stroke / TIA or adult at risk of stroke.
- OSA diagnosis is based on objective sleep testing rather than symptom label alone.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Whether CPAP reliably prevents recurrent stroke remains unproven in randomized trial evidence.
- CSA-related stroke risk is not well studied in this source.
- PFO-related paradoxical embolism mechanism in OSA is plausible but not established as routine causal attribution.

### Concept: Poststroke Sleep-Related Breathing Disorders Screening and Diagnosis

#### One-Sentence Definition

Poststroke sleep-related breathing disorder screening is a high-suspicion workflow that uses clinical risk cues, early overnight oximetry, and formal polysomnography or HSAT to detect OSA, CSA or Cheyne-Stokes breathing after stroke / TIA.

#### Known Facts

- Sleep-related breathing disorders include OSA, CSA and Cheyne-Stokes breathing.
- In stroke / TIA patients, OSA is more common than CSA; a meta-analysis found CSA or Cheyne-Stokes breathing as predominant abnormality in 7 percent.
- In a meta-analysis of over 14,000 stroke / TIA patients, acute phase pooled prevalence was 67 percent for AHI >=5, 50 percent for AHI >=15 and 32 percent for AHI >=30.
- Severe sleep apnea prevalence in chronic poststroke / TIA phase was 25 percent in seven studies.
- Sleep-related breathing disorders can be detectable within 24 hours after stroke.
- Typical symptoms such as daytime sleepiness and snoring may be absent after stroke.
- Clinical features associated with higher likelihood include increased BMI, male sex, systolic hypertension, early neurologic deterioration, nocturnal oxygen desaturation, increased stroke severity, hemorrhagic stroke, prior stroke and AF.
- Berlin questionnaire showed only moderate diagnostic utility in poststroke patients; modified STOP-Bang without neck circumference had high sensitivity but low specificity.
- Diagnosis requires formal sleep testing with polysomnography or HSAT type III / IV.
- OSA is defined by >=5 predominantly obstructive respiratory events per hour of sleep or recording time; stroke patients need not have additional symptoms for diagnostic criteria.
- CSA requires >=5 central apneas / hypopneas per hour and >50 percent central events, plus related symptoms.
- At minimum, source recommends reviewing overnight oximetry during the first five days after stroke.
- Frequent nocturnal oxygen desaturations, dysphagia or dysphonia increase suspicion.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
stroke / preexisting undiagnosed OSA or CSA
-> impaired consciousness, sedatives, bulbar / infratentorial / bilateral lesion effects
-> obstructive, central or Cheyne-Stokes events during sleep
-> intermittent hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation
-> early neurologic deterioration, poorer rehabilitation tolerance, worse cognitive / functional outcome signal
```

#### Inferences

- Normal speech about "no daytime sleepiness" is insufficient to exclude sleep apnea after stroke.
- Overnight oximetry is a pragmatic inpatient screen, but post-discharge sleep testing remains necessary when risk persists.

#### Assumptions

- Clinical team can access oximetry, HSAT or polysomnography and interpret results in context.
- Patient goals and capacity to tolerate PAP therapy are relevant to test timing.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Universal diagnostic sleep testing after stroke remains debated because of resources and adherence feasibility.
- AHI / RDI may change during recovery, requiring repeat testing.

### Concept: Poststroke PAP Treatment and Prognosis Boundary

#### One-Sentence Definition

Poststroke PAP treatment boundary is the principle that PAP and behavioral treatment are mainstays after objective sleep-related breathing disorder diagnosis, but stroke-specific outcome benefit is less certain than physiologic apnea reduction and must be reassessed as stroke recovery changes respiratory pattern and tolerance.

#### Known Facts

- PAP therapy and behavioral modifications are mainstays of treatment for diagnosed sleep-related breathing disorders.
- Acute stroke treatment decisions should be tailored to clinical status and perceived ability to adhere to PAP therapy.
- Hypnotics and benzodiazepine derivatives can exacerbate sleep-disordered breathing and should generally be avoided when relevant.
- PAP mode depends on the predominant sleep-disordered breathing type.
- In OSA, behavioral modifications including weight loss are recommended.
- Oral appliance efficacy in poststroke OSA has not been studied.
- The source repeats testing at three-month intervals during the first year when possible because AHI / RDI may change during recovery.
- Sleep-disordered breathing may improve as stroke improves; therapy may not always be indefinite, though it is generally required into the rehabilitation phase.
- CSA in the context of Cheyne-Stokes breathing is more likely self-limited than OSA after stroke.
- CSA may be more prevalent during the first five days after acute stroke; auto-titrating CPAP may be poorly tolerated if central apneas are prevalent.
- Ticagrelor has been associated with shift from OSA to CSA, potentially reducing auto-titrating CPAP tolerance.
- Stroke-specific treatment evidence is limited; a 2023 meta-analysis of seven randomized trials found nonsignificant trend toward fewer new nonfatal vascular events with CPAP.
- Observational studies have found lower vascular events among CPAP users, but trial evidence is mixed and long-term follow-up limited.
- Poor PAP adherence after stroke may relate to tolerance, motivation, cognitive deficits, age and neglect.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
objective OSA / CSA diagnosis
-> select PAP / BPAP / oxygen / behavioral route according to event type and clinical status
-> reduce respiratory events and nocturnal desaturation
-> possible better sleep, BP, cognition and rehab tolerance
-> uncertain magnitude for recurrent vascular event reduction
```

#### Inferences

- PAP trial should be treated as an actively monitored intervention, not an automatic discharge checkbox.
- In acute stroke, PAP intolerance should trigger reassessment of CSA, mask fit, cognition, neglect, medication effects and perfusion concerns.

#### Assumptions

- OSA / CSA subtype has been objectively characterized or is being monitored during a pragmatic acute trial.
- Rehab team can coordinate sleep medicine, respiratory therapy and caregiver support.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- CPAP effect on recurrent stroke and long-term functional outcome remains uncertain.
- The source does not provide detailed PAP titration protocols or definitive pharmacologic weight-loss criteria for poststroke patients.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Poststroke sleep apnea cannot be excluded by absence of snoring or daytime sleepiness.
- In acute stroke, review overnight oximetry during the first five days when possible; frequent desaturation, dysphagia or dysphonia should lower the threshold for formal testing.
- Formal diagnosis uses polysomnography or HSAT type III / IV; oximetry is a triage tool, not full diagnostic replacement.
- PAP is reasonable after objective diagnosis, but adherence and stroke-specific outcome benefit require monitoring.
- CSA and Cheyne-Stokes breathing may be more acute and self-limited; they can change PAP mode choice and tolerance.
- Ticagrelor-associated CSA signal is relevant when auto-titrating CPAP is poorly tolerated after stroke.

## Research-Useful Points

- Separate incident stroke risk evidence from poststroke recovery / recurrence evidence.
- Distinguish OSA, CSA and Cheyne-Stokes breathing in stroke cohorts; mixed labels can hide treatment-response differences.
- Report timing after stroke, test modality, AHI / RDI threshold, PAP adherence and functional outcome window.
- Avoid treating observational CPAP associations as proof of recurrent stroke prevention.
