# Source Summary: Nurturing Care Practice Guide

## Source Type

- WHO / UNICEF practice guide.
- Full title: *Nurturing care practice guide: strengthening nurturing care through health and nutrition services*.
- Publication year: 2022.
- Focus: implementation ideas for managers and service providers in maternal, newborn, child health, and nutrition services.

## Reliability Level

- Tier 2 in this wiki: official WHO / UNICEF implementation guide, but not a formal guideline, textbook chapter, or systematic review.
- Strongest for operationalizing nurturing care in existing health and nutrition services.
- Weaker for causal effect size claims, because this guide synthesizes and applies prior frameworks and resources rather than presenting a new evidence review.
- It explicitly does not replace skills training; it functions as an entry point, review aid, and service-adaptation guide.

## One-Sentence Summary

The guide explains how maternal, newborn, child health, and nutrition services can embed nurturing care into routine contacts by strengthening responsive caregiving, early learning opportunities, safety and security, and caregiver well-being.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Integrating nurturing care into health and nutrition services

#### One-Sentence Definition

Integrating nurturing care into health and nutrition services means using routine antenatal, postnatal, well-child, sick-child, inpatient, home-visit, and nutritional rehabilitation contacts to coach caregivers in responsive interaction, early learning, safety, and self-care.

#### Known Facts

- Nurturing care has five interrelated components: good health, adequate nutrition, safety and security, opportunities for early learning, and responsive caregiving.
- The guide focuses on responsive caregiving, early learning, safety and security, and caregiver well-being because these are often less visible in health and nutrition services.
- Responsive caregiving is defined as the caregiver's ability to notice, understand, and respond to child signals in a timely and appropriate way.
- Responsive caregiving supports illness recognition, feeding, protection from injury and adversity, learning interactions, trust, and social relationships.
- Early learning is not limited to preschool or formal academics; it occurs through everyday interactions, play, routines, and communication from pregnancy onward.
- Safety and security include physical safety, emotional safety, environmental risks, food and water access, and protection from neglect, abuse, violence, and harsh discipline.
- Caregiver physical and mental health affects the caregiver's ability to provide nurturing care.
- Health and nutrition service providers see many caregivers and children frequently and can reinforce caregiving practices during routine contacts.
- Managers can support nurturing care by making facilities accessible and welcoming, adapting protocols, building provider capacity, reducing caregiver-child separation, and mapping referral resources.
- Providers need interpersonal communication skills: asking open-ended questions, listening, observing, praising, empathizing, shared problem-solving, and coaching new skills.
- Service contacts that can support nurturing care include antenatal care, postnatal care, well-child visits, home visits, sick-child care, inpatient care, and nutritional rehabilitation.
- The guide uses three support levels: universal support for all families, targeted support for at-risk families / communities, and indicated support for children or caregivers with additional needs.
- Developmental milestone assessment alone is unlikely to be effective if referral networks and indicated supports are weak.
- Nutritional rehabilitation can incorporate responsive feeding, play, communication, and caregiver support; play is described as part of severe wasting management guidance since WHO 1999.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Routine health / nutrition contact
-> provider observes caregiver-child interaction and caregiver stress
-> provider models, praises, coaches, and problem-solves responsive caregiving / play / safety / self-care
-> caregiver gains practical, context-matched caregiving skills
-> child receives more responsive, stimulating, safe, and emotionally buffered care
-> healthier growth, development, illness recovery, and family functioning
```

#### Inferences

- A pediatric service that only checks weight, vaccines, symptoms, or medications misses a major opportunity to influence early development.
- Waiting rooms, growth monitoring, breastfeeding observation, immunization, home visits, and discharge planning can become developmental intervention moments if staff are trained and workflow allows it.
- Service design is part of the intervention: rooming-in, zero separation, play corners, referral maps, supervisor checklists, and provider mentoring shape whether caregivers can actually practise nurturing care.

#### Assumptions

- Service providers have enough time, supervision, and practical tools to coach caregivers rather than only deliver information.
- Referral pathways exist, or managers are actively mapping and advocating for them.
- Suggestions are locally adapted for language, culture, disability access, humanitarian constraints, and available resources.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- The guide gives examples and implementation ideas rather than graded recommendations.
- Feasibility varies by staffing, facility design, local resources, referral capacity, and crisis context.
- It does not fully describe specialist services for developmental disabilities, mental health, rehabilitation, child protection, or chronic illness.
- Causal claims about long-term outcomes depend on underlying evidence cited by the guide, not on new analyses in the guide itself.

## Clinically Useful Points

- During antenatal care, providers can ask about caregiver feelings, concerns, fetal movement, father / other caregiver involvement, smoke exposure, safe water, and home preparation.
- During postnatal care, breastfeeding observation can also be used to coach eye contact, soft voice, touch, hunger cue recognition, and father participation.
- During well-child care, providers can ask what the child learned since the last visit, whether any skill was lost, how caregivers play / talk, and how they manage screen time, sleep, feeding, and safety hazards.
- During sick-child care, responsive caregiving should be linked to recognizing illness signs, feeding, giving medicine, soothing, and follow-up.
- During hospitalization, avoiding separation and supporting caregiver participation can protect interaction, play, feeding, comfort, and discharge readiness.
- During nutritional rehabilitation, responsive feeding, play, and communication are not extra decorations; they support appetite, acceptance of food, growth, and development.

## Research-Useful Points

- The guide highlights implementation needs: training, mentoring, provider turnover, facility accessibility, caregiver-friendly workflow, referral mapping, and crisis adaptation.
- It also points to measurement problems: service systems need ways to observe caregiver-child interaction, caregiver mental health, development concerns, and referral completion.
