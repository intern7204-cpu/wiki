# Source Summary: Oropharyngeal dysphagia: Clinical features, diagnosis, and management

## One-Sentence Summary

Adult oropharyngeal dysphagia is an alarm symptom defined by difficulty transferring food from mouth to pharynx and initiating swallowing; evaluation must distinguish esophageal dysphagia and globus, identify neuromuscular / structural / cricopharyngeal etiologies, assess aspiration severity with appropriate FEES / VFSS / manometry, and manage by treating the cause plus selected swallow rehabilitation, nutrition support, endoscopic/surgical intervention, or specialized procedures.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Adult oropharyngeal dysphagia clinical pathway

#### One-Sentence Definition

Adult oropharyngeal dysphagia clinical pathway 是以 symptom timing、neck-level obstruction sensation、airway/nutrition risk 與 etiologic clues，將 swallowing problem 分成 oropharyngeal、esophageal 或 globus，再用 targeted examination and instrumental testing 決定 management 的流程。

#### Known Facts

- Dysphagia is a subjective sensation of difficulty or abnormality of swallowing.
- Oropharyngeal or transfer dysphagia is characterized by difficulty initiating a swallow.
- Oropharyngeal dysphagia may be accompanied by nasopharyngeal regurgitation, aspiration, and residual food sensation in the pharynx.
- Esophageal dysphagia is characterized by difficulty swallowing several seconds after initiating a swallow and food-stuck sensation.
- Typical symptoms include difficulty transferring food from mouth to pharynx, neck-level obstruction sensation, coughing, choking, drooling, and regurgitation with liquids or solids.
- Patients may have aspiration pneumonia history and weight loss.
- Oropharyngeal dysphagia is an alarm symptom that warrants urgent evaluation.
- Evaluation should identify underlying etiology and assess severity of oropharyngeal dysfunction and aspiration before treatment.
- Initial evaluation includes oral cavity, head/neck, supraclavicular and neurologic examination, especially cranial nerves involved in swallowing.
- Red-flag etiologic clues include older age with alcohol use disorder, smoking, blood in mouth or weight loss suggesting malignancy; otalgia suggesting hypopharyngeal or head/neck lesion; dry mouth/eyes or xerogenic medications; speech/voice change suggesting neuromuscular dysfunction or vocal cord paralysis; regurgitation/halitosis/neck fullness/pneumonia suggesting Zenker's diverticulum; late-meal dysphagia suggesting myasthenia gravis.
- For suspected neuromuscular disease, the source performs videofluoroscopic modified barium swallow and esophageal manometry.
- For no evidence of systemic disease, the source begins with FEES and proceeds to VFSS/manometry if no etiology is found.
- VFSS visualizes the rapid sequence of swallowing events, laryngeal penetration, aspiration severity, bolus movement, pooling, hyoid/laryngeal elevation, UES relaxation, and pharyngeal contraction.
- FEES evaluates pharyngeal/laryngeal structure and function at bedside, visualizes secretions, and may identify structural abnormalities; it requires specialized training.
- FEES and VFSS correlate, but FEES may rate penetration/aspiration risk as more severe; clinical significance of this difference is uncertain.
- Manometry of the UES quantifies pressures and timing of pharyngeal contraction and deglutitive UES relaxation; it rarely changes management but can help identify etiology and candidates for myotomy.
- Management goals are to improve food transfer and prevent aspiration.
- Mild oropharyngeal dysphagia after stroke, head/neck trauma, surgery, or degenerative neurologic disease is treated with swallow rehabilitation to facilitate oral intake, but evidence is limited and recommendations are Grade 2C in the source.
- Severe dysfunction with aspiration risk may require enteral nutrition.
- Primary cricopharyngeal dysfunction may be treated with endoscopic or open myotomy; botulinum toxin injection is reserved for non-surgical/non-dilation candidates in expertise centers.
- Neuromuscular electrical stimulation has mixed evidence; further studies are needed.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Oropharyngeal / neuromuscular / structural / UES disorder
-> impaired bolus formation, propulsion, airway closure, pharyngeal clearance, or UES opening
-> difficulty initiating swallow + residue / penetration / aspiration
-> coughing, choking, regurgitation, aspiration pneumonia, weight loss, diet restriction
-> urgent etiologic and severity assessment
-> targeted rehabilitation, nutrition route decision, endoscopic/surgical procedure, or disease-specific treatment
```

#### Inferences

- Adult dysphagia workup should begin with timing and symptom-context sorting before choosing VFSS, FEES, manometry, or ENT/GI testing.
- A diet-only plan without etiologic evaluation may miss malignancy, Zenker's diverticulum, vocal cord paralysis, myasthenia gravis, inflammatory myopathy, Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, or structural lesion.
- Instrumental assessment should be selected by the question being asked: physiology/timing, structural lesion, secretion burden, aspiration severity, or UES pressure/relaxation.

#### Assumptions

- Adult patient with suspected oropharyngeal dysphagia.
- Clinical setting can access speech-language pathology, otolaryngology, gastroenterology, VFSS/modified barium swallow, FEES, and manometry when indicated.
- Acute airway compromise, unstable pneumonia, severe dehydration, or inability to maintain nutrition is handled urgently before elective workup.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This source is an expert topic review and cites limited evidence for many swallow rehabilitation techniques.
- VFSS parameters are not fully standardized and interobserver reliability is best for penetration/aspiration rather than all physiologic details.
- FEES/VFSS differences in perceived aspiration severity have uncertain clinical significance.
- Cricopharyngeal myotomy evidence is largely case series/systematic review of nonrandomized data; complications exist.
- NMES evidence is heterogeneous and its role remains unclear.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Oropharyngeal dysphagia is urgent/alarm-level until structural, neurologic, infectious, metabolic, or malignant causes are considered.
- History should sort difficulty initiating swallow versus food-stuck several seconds later versus globus symptoms between meals.
- Ask about coughing/choking, nasal regurgitation, drooling, voice change, weak cough, dysarthria, weight loss, aspiration pneumonia, xerostomia, medication effects, intubation history, malignancy risk, Zenker's symptoms, and fatigue-late-in-meal pattern.
- Exam should include oral/head/neck/supraclavicular assessment and cranial nerve-focused neurologic examination.
- VFSS is strong for dynamic bolus/physiology analysis; FEES is strong for bedside structural/secretion assessment; manometry is most useful when UES dysfunction or myotomy candidacy is the question.
- Swallow maneuvers and diet modification should be matched to the physiologic defect rather than prescribed generically.
- Enteral nutrition is appropriate when oral nutrition is not tolerated or aspiration risk remains high despite measures.
- Cricopharyngeal intervention requires careful selection; dysarthria predicts poor response to myotomy in the source.

## Research-Useful Points

- Standardization and interobserver reliability of VFSS parameters remain important research/quality targets.
- FEES versus VFSS prediction differences for aspiration need outcome-linked clarification.
- Swallow rehabilitation evidence is biologically plausible but limited, often based on small or maneuver-specific studies.
- NMES evidence is heterogeneous and needs better phenotype/protocol-specific trials.
- Cricopharyngeal myotomy, dilation, and botulinum toxin require better comparative and long-term outcome data.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with treating oropharyngeal dysphagia as merely a nursing diet-order problem.
- Conflicts with using FEES, VFSS, or manometry interchangeably without specifying the clinical question.
- Conflicts with treating globus sensation as dysphagia when symptoms occur independent of swallowing and diagnostic criteria are otherwise met.
- Conflicts with assuming thickened liquids are universally beneficial; source ties bolus viscosity and diet modification to specific physiologic problems.
- Conflicts with treating botulinum toxin injection for cricopharyngeal dysfunction as routine low-risk therapy.
