# Source Summary: Currier et al. 2023 - Resistance Training Prescription for Strength and Hypertrophy in Healthy Adults

## Source Type

- Citation: Currier BS, McLeod JC, Banfield L, et al. *Resistance training prescription for muscle strength and hypertrophy in healthy adults: a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis*. Br J Sports Med. 2023;57:1211-1220.
- DOI: `10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807`
- Study type: systematic review + Bayesian network meta-analysis.
- Source scope: randomized trials of supervised resistance training in generally healthy, community-dwelling adults.

## Reliability Level

- Source tier: Tier 3, because this is a systematic review / meta-analysis.
- Evidence strength: moderate for the broad direction that supervised resistance training improves strength and hypertrophy in healthy adults compared with no exercise.
- Confidence is lower for exact ranking of individual prescriptions because risk of bias was commonly moderate to high, several nodes were sparse, and load / set / frequency were coded categorically.

## One-Sentence Summary

In healthy adults, most supervised resistance training prescriptions improve strength and hypertrophy compared with no exercise; higher-load multiset prescriptions rank highest for strength, whereas multiple-set prescriptions rank highest for hypertrophy, but exact rankings should not be treated as universal clinical rules.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Resistance Training Load / Set / Frequency for Strength and Hypertrophy

#### One-Sentence Definition

Resistance training prescription can be operationally coded by load, sets, and weekly frequency to compare how different combinations affect strength and hypertrophy in healthy adults.

#### Known Facts

- The review searched MEDLINE, Embase, Emcare, SPORTDiscus, CINAHL, and Web of Science through 7 February 2022.
- Eligible trials included randomized studies in healthy adults aged 18 years or older, comparing at least two predefined conditions and measuring muscle strength, muscle size, or physical function.
- Excluded populations included athletes, military personnel, people with comorbidities or injury, hospitalised / rehabilitation patients, long-term care residents, explicitly obese or overweight participants, and unsupervised home-based RT.
- RTx was coded as `XY#`:
  - `X`: load, `H` = at least 80% 1RM, `L` = below 80% 1RM.
  - `Y`: sets, `M` = multiset, `S` = single set.
  - `#`: weekly frequency, `1`, `2`, or `3` for at least 3 days/week.
- Strength NMA included 178 studies and 5097 participants.
- Hypertrophy NMA included 119 studies and 3364 participants.
- Compared with CTRL, strength posterior SMDs ranged from 0.75 to 1.60, with the largest estimate for `HM3`.
- Compared with CTRL, hypertrophy posterior SMDs ranged from 0.10 to 0.66, with the largest estimate for `HM2`.
- For between-RTx comparisons, most 95% CrI included zero: 13.6% of strength comparisons and 2.2% of hypertrophy comparisons had 95% CrI excluding zero.
- Strength top-three probabilities were highest for `HM3`, `HM2`, and `HM1`.
- Hypertrophy top-three probabilities were highest for `HM2`, `LM1`, and `LM2`.
- Network meta-regression did not show obvious modifying effects from age, training status, proportion female, duration, volitional fatigue, relative weekly volume load, measurement tool / region, or publication year.
- Physical function evidence was sparse and limited mainly to older healthy adults, with no clear differences between RT prescriptions.
- The authors state that effects of different RTx on health outcomes remain largely unknown.

#### Mechanism Chain

1. Resistance training exposes muscle and neuromuscular systems to repeated force production demands.
2. Higher loads increase specificity for maximal strength testing and likely increase neural / high-force adaptation demands.
3. Multiple sets and higher training volume increase cumulative hypertrophic stimulus.
4. Load, sets, and frequency interact as a prescription package; they should not be interpreted as independent isolated variables.
5. In a network meta-analysis, direct and indirect randomized comparisons estimate the relative effect of each RTx node.
6. Rankings can suggest plausible best-performing nodes, but sparse nodes, risk of bias, and transitivity assumptions limit certainty.

#### Inferences

- For healthy adults whose main goal is maximal strength, higher-load multiset RT is a reasonable optimization direction when safe and tolerated.
- For healthy adults whose main goal is hypertrophy, multiple sets appear more consistently important than load alone.
- Lower-load RT can still improve strength compared with no exercise and should not be dismissed when preference, pain, safety, access, or adherence limit higher-load training.
- The clinical priority for many adults may be sustained engagement in any RTx rather than perfect optimization of load, sets, and frequency.

#### Assumptions

- The `80% 1RM` threshold is a useful operational split between higher-load and lower-load RT.
- Trial-level group means can support broad prescription inference for community-dwelling healthy adults.
- Strength and hypertrophy outcomes measured by different tools can be meaningfully compared with standardized mean differences.
- Direct and indirect comparisons in the NMA satisfy enough transitivity for ranking to be useful.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Risk of bias was moderate to high in many included studies.
- Several RTx nodes were sparse, which widened credible intervals and reduced certainty.
- The framework could not include periodized programs or overlapping load prescriptions such as 60-90% 1RM.
- Load, sets, and frequency were categorical rather than continuous dose-response variables.
- Inter-set rest, time under tension, repetition velocity, tempo, and volitional fatigue were not fully integrated into RTx nodes.
- Results should not be directly generalized to elite athletes, special populations, chronic disease, inpatient rehabilitation, frailty, or unsupervised home programs.
- Health outcomes beyond strength and hypertrophy remain uncertain.

## Clinically Useful Points

- For healthy adults, some supervised RT is usually better than no RT for strength and hypertrophy.
- If optimizing strength, prioritize tolerable higher-load training and multiple sets when clinically appropriate.
- If optimizing hypertrophy, prioritize enough sets / volume; high load is not mandatory for all hypertrophy goals.
- Do not use this paper alone to prescribe RT for patients with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, osteoarthritis, acute injury, inpatient rehab needs, frailty, obesity/overweight, or other excluded contexts.
- Do not frame `HM2` or `HM3` as mandatory; preference, time constraints, symptom response, technique, and safety remain legitimate reasons to select another RTx.

## Research-Useful Points

- The paper is useful as a structured example of multivariate exercise prescription comparison using Bayesian NMA.
- The `XY#` coding framework is clear but intentionally coarse.
- Future work needs model-based or dose-response NMA to treat load, sets, and frequency as continuous variables.
- Future work should better report and analyze rest interval, tempo, repetition velocity, time under tension, proximity to failure, and movement specificity.
- For clinical rehab, separate population-specific evidence is required before translating healthy-adult RTx rankings.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflict with "high load is always required for hypertrophy": this source suggests multiple sets / volume are more salient for hypertrophy rankings, and many loads can promote hypertrophy.
- Conflict with "training to failure is necessary": network meta-regression for volitional fatigue did not improve model fit or substantially alter estimates, though this does not rule out importance in trained individuals.
- Conflict with "there is one optimal RT prescription": most between-RTx comparisons had 95% CrI containing zero, and adherence / preference remain central.
- Conflict with clinical extrapolation: special populations and rehabilitation patients were excluded, so this source cannot replace disease-specific exercise safety guidance.
