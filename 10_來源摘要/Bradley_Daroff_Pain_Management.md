## One-Sentence Summary

Chronic and neuropathic pain should be understood as a nervous-system modulation problem involving peripheral input, central sensitization, descending control, psychology, function, and selective medication / procedure use rather than as a pain-score-only problem.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Peripheral and central sensitization

#### One-Sentence Definition

Sensitization is a pain amplification process in which peripheral nociceptors, dorsal horn circuits, descending modulation, and central representation make minor or absent stimuli produce disproportionate pain.

#### Known Facts

- Acute pain usually signals tissue damage and tends to resolve with healing; chronic pain may persist beyond normal healing and can lose its protective role.
- A-delta fibers carry fast, sharp, well-localized pain; C fibers carry slower, dull, burning, aching, and poorly localized pain.
- Small-fiber neuropathy can present with neuropathic pain despite normal nerve conduction studies.
- The chapter describes neuronal plasticity through activation / autosensitization / wind-up, modulation / heterosensitization / central sensitization, and longer-term modification / gene alteration.
- Descending inhibition includes opioid, noradrenergic, and serotonergic mechanisms.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Tissue or neural injury
→ nociceptor activation and inflammatory mediators
→ dorsal horn wind-up and central sensitization
→ descending inhibition / facilitation imbalance
→ pain from minor stimuli or after stimulus removal
→ chronic pain, disability, sleep and mood burden
```

#### Inferences

- A normal MRI or NCS does not exclude clinically meaningful pain amplification, especially when small fibers or central sensitization are involved.
- Treatment that only targets peripheral tissue may fail when central gain has become a major driver.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- The chapter explains sensitization mechanisms, but it does not provide a single clinical test that definitively quantifies central sensitization in routine practice.

### Concept: Neuropathic pain medication hierarchy

#### One-Sentence Definition

Neuropathic pain pharmacology should usually begin with adjuvant analgesics such as TCAs, SNRIs, and gabapentinoids, while long-term opioid use in nonmalignant chronic pain is discouraged unless first-line options fail and risks are managed.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter identifies TCAs and anticonvulsants as first-line options for neuropathic pain.
- If one class fails, adding a second class is described as reasonable.
- SSRIs are described as less effective than TCAs for pain; SNRIs such as venlafaxine, duloxetine, and milnacipran have analgesic evidence.
- Gabapentin and pregabalin are highlighted for neuropathic pain; pregabalin has FDA-approved indications including diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, and fibromyalgia.
- Lidocaine / mexiletine and topical lidocaine / capsaicin are discussed as later-line or focal options.
- Opioids are controversial in chronic nonmalignant pain because of tolerance, physical dependence, addiction risk, and opioid-induced hyperalgesia.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Neuropathic pain phenotype
→ choose medication class by mechanism and adverse-effect profile
→ titrate slowly and monitor function, sleep, cognition, mood, and safety
→ reserve opioid escalation for selected refractory cases with risk mitigation
```

#### Inferences

- In rehabilitation notes, medication response should be tracked by function and participation, not only by pain score.
- Electric, lancinating, burning, or allodynic pain should prompt a neuropathic medication review before repeated passive modalities.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Dose examples in the chapter may not reflect newer safety alerts, geriatric prescribing standards, renal dosing, pregnancy, or drug-interaction guidance.

### Concept: Multidisciplinary pain management

#### One-Sentence Definition

Multidisciplinary pain care combines medication, rehabilitation, psychological therapy, procedures, nutrition, vocational planning, and self-management rather than serially trying one passive treatment at a time.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter lists medication, PT / OT, TENS, bracing, CBT, biofeedback, relaxation, interventional procedures, implantable therapies, complementary treatment, nutrition, and vocational services as components of pain management.
- It describes simultaneous multimodal treatment as preferable to a purely sequential approach.
- Rehabilitation goals include functional recovery, quality of life, and overcoming deconditioning.
- Modalities are described as temporary adjuncts; active components include stretching, aerobic exercise, work conditioning, strength training, orthotics, and prosthetics.

#### Inferences

- A pain plan without functional restoration or psychological / behavioral support is usually incomplete for chronic pain.
- Passive symptom relief is clinically useful when it enables graded activity and participation, not when it replaces them.

### Concept: CRPS as a clinical syndrome with weak treatment evidence

#### One-Sentence Definition

CRPS is regional continuous pain after trauma with sensory, autonomic, sudomotor, motor, and trophic features that is diagnosed clinically after excluding mimics, while treatment evidence remains limited.

#### Known Facts

- CRPS type I occurs without a definable nerve lesion; CRPS type II occurs with a definable nerve lesion.
- Pain is regional, disproportionate, and not restricted to a single peripheral nerve territory.
- Features include pain, edema, temperature / color / sudomotor changes, motor dysfunction, and psychological burden.
- Diagnosis is clinical and requires exclusion of conditions such as infection and DVT.
- EMG / NCS are not sensitive for CRPS and may be painful.
- Treatment goals include pain relief, function, and psychological care.
- The chapter describes early PT / OT and psychological evaluation after prolonged symptoms; evidence is weak for many interventions.

#### Inferences

- CRPS should not be reduced to "sympathetic pain" only; the chapter's mechanism list includes neurogenic inflammation, immune mechanisms, sympathetic coupling, central sensitization, and cortical reorganization.
- Early function-preserving rehabilitation is more defensible than waiting for complete pain resolution.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Distinguish acute protective pain from chronic pain as a disease state.
- Track neuropathic pain features, allodynia, function, mood, sleep, opioid exposure, and adverse effects.
- Avoid chronic meperidine use because of normeperidine seizure risk.
- Be cautious with tramadol in patients taking TCAs or at seizure risk.
- Spinal cord stimulation requires a trial before permanent implantation; the chapter uses more than 50% pain reduction and satisfactory coverage as a trial-success threshold.
- Trigeminal neuralgia is classically paroxysmal unilateral electric facial pain with trigger zones; MRI is part of initial evaluation and carbamazepine is first-choice medication in the chapter.

## Research-Useful Points

- Central sensitization and plasticity can explain discordance between tissue findings and pain intensity.
- Future concept pages should separate peripheral neuropathy, central poststroke pain, SCI pain, CRPS, phantom limb pain, and trigeminal neuralgia rather than treating all as one pain category.

## Conflicts / Cautions

- The chapter includes CDC 2016 opioid guideline framing; opioid policy and dosing thresholds should be updated with current guideline sources before being used for clinical decision support.
- Interventional pain procedures require procedure-specific, anatomy-specific, and guideline-level sources before recommendation.
