## One-Sentence Summary

Cervicocephalic artery dissection 是 young stroke 的重要原因但可發生於任何年齡；臨床 suspicion 由 acute/subacute head or neck pain、Horner syndrome、cranial/cervical neuropathy、pulsatile tinnitus、TIA/stroke 或 SAH 線索啟動，最後必須以 head/neck MRI/MRA 或 CT/CTA 等 neuroimaging 確認。

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Cervicocephalic Artery Dissection 臨床辨識

#### One-Sentence Definition

Cervicocephalic artery dissection 的臨床辨識，是把 acute/subacute head or neck pain、local compressive symptoms、ischemic territory symptoms、SAH pattern、minor trigger and predisposition 放在一起判斷是否需要 urgent vascular imaging。

#### Known Facts

- Dissection occurs when arterial wall integrity is compromised and blood collects between wall layers as an intramural hematoma.
- Arterial dissection is a common cause of stroke in young adults but may occur at any age.
- Mean age in North American and European studies was 44 to 53 years.
- A population-based study reported combined annual incidence of spontaneous cervical artery dissection as 2.6 per 100,000.
- Summary section states cervical and cerebral artery dissection occurs in approximately 3 cases per 100,000 and accounts for up to one quarter of strokes in the young.
- Extracranial dissection is more frequent than intracranial dissection in North America and Europe, but intracranial dissection appears more common in Asian populations and children in several case series.
- Minor or trivial trauma / mechanical events are reported triggers in up to 40 percent of cervical artery dissection cases.
- "Spontaneous" dissection does not mean no trigger or predisposition.
- Head and/or neck pain is the most frequent initial symptom, found in 57 to 90 percent of cases.
- Pain is often severe, continuous, recent-onset, and may localize to temple/eye/cheek/teeth in carotid dissection or occiput in vertebral artery dissection.
- Horner syndrome occurs in approximately 25 percent of cases and internal carotid artery dissection typically causes partial Horner syndrome with ptosis and miosis but not anhidrosis.
- Carotid dissection can cause single or multiple compressive cranial neuropathies in up to 12 percent of cases; cranial nerve XII is most common.
- Pulsatile tinnitus was present in 8 percent in the CADISP study.
- In one population-based report, cerebral ischemia was noted in 67 percent, with TIA and cerebral infarction in 23 and 56 percent.
- Carotid artery dissection may cause anterior circulation stroke syndrome, transient monocular blindness, retinal artery occlusion or ischemic optic neuropathy.
- Vertebral artery dissection may cause lateral medullary infarction, other posterior circulation stroke syndromes or cervical spinal cord ischemia.
- Intracranial artery dissection may result in SAH; a 2015 systematic review of retrospective series reported SAH association in 8 to 69 percent of intracranial dissection cases.
- Patients age 60 years or older with cervical artery dissection may be less likely to have neck pain, headache, preceding trauma or mechanical trigger.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
arterial wall vulnerability or trigger
-> wall-layer separation and intramural hematoma
-> subintimal dissection: stenosis / occlusion
-> thromboembolism more than hypoperfusion
-> TIA or ischemic stroke
```

```text
subadventitial dissection / pseudoaneurysm
-> vessel dilatation or adjacent neural compression
-> head/neck/facial pain, partial Horner syndrome, cranial neuropathy, cervical root symptoms or pulsatile tinnitus
```

```text
intracranial dissection with thin adventitial support
-> vessel rupture in a minority
-> subarachnoid hemorrhage
```

#### Inferences

- A young or middle-aged stroke patient with new ipsilateral head/neck pain should trigger dissection consideration even if traditional vascular risk factors are present.
- Acute Horner syndrome plus ipsilateral internal carotid territory ischemia is a high-yield pattern, but absence of Horner syndrome does not lower risk enough to stop evaluation.
- Vertebral artery dissection should be considered when posterior circulation symptoms coexist with neck/occipital pain, but dizziness/vertigo alone is not specific.
- "No trauma" should not be used to dismiss dissection because many triggers are minor or forgotten.

#### Assumptions

- Adult acute/subacute stroke or headache diagnostic setting.
- Local system can obtain urgent head/neck vascular imaging.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Many associations with rare connective tissue disorders are observational and uncommon.
- Cervical manipulation therapy may trigger dissection, but causality and absolute incidence remain difficult to establish.
- Dizziness/vertigo is common in vertebral artery dissection reports, but the source states ischemic causality is uncertain in a systematic review.
- This source does not resolve antithrombotic choice or prognosis.

### Concept: Cervicocephalic Artery Dissection 影像確認與 Pitfalls

#### One-Sentence Definition

Dissection diagnosis is confirmed by neuroimaging evidence of long tapered stenosis/occlusion, pseudoaneurysm, intimal flap, double lumen or intramural hematoma, usually with urgent MRI/MRA or CT/CTA of the head and neck.

#### Known Facts

- Clinical features may raise suspicion, but diagnosis is confirmed by neuroimaging.
- The source obtains urgent noninvasive multimodal imaging with brain/neck MRI plus head/neck MRA, or head CT plus head/neck CTA.
- MRI should include standard axial T1, T2, FLAIR and DWI sequences.
- Cervical and cranial T1-weighted MRI with fat-saturation is useful for small intramural hemorrhage.
- MRA should include contrast-enhanced and time-of-flight MRA.
- CT/CTA is an alternative when appropriate and available.
- There is no single gold standard; diagnosis often requires complementary imaging modalities and may require repeat imaging over time.
- DSA is reserved for younger patients when clinical suspicion remains high despite negative noninvasive imaging.
- DSA is not needed if CTA or MRA clearly establishes cerebral or cervical artery dissection.
- A 2009 systematic review found MRI/MRA and CT/CTA sensitivity and specificity for cervicocephalic dissection were relatively similar.
- Carotid duplex and TCD may screen or monitor, but carotid duplex detects abnormalities in only 68 to 95 percent of cases.
- Duplex / TCD have suboptimal yield near the skull base and in vertebral artery dissection within transverse foramina.
- Ultrasound is unreliable for carotid artery dissection with isolated Horner syndrome.
- Ultrasound-negative but clinically suggestive cases should proceed to MRA or CTA.
- Characteristic angiographic findings include string sign, elongated/tapered stenosis or flame-shaped occlusion, intimal flap, pseudoaneurysm and intramural hematoma crescent / yin-yang sign.
- In a population-based 48-patient cervical artery dissection study, diagnostic patterns were elongated tapered stenosis 48 percent, tapered occlusion 35 percent and pseudoaneurysm 17 percent.
- In a prospective European vertebral artery dissection study, intramural hematoma was observed on MRI in 91 percent of 157 dissections.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
intramural blood or false lumen
-> vessel-wall signal abnormality and/or lumen contour abnormality
-> MRI/MRA or CT/CTA shows wall hematoma, stenosis, occlusion, intimal flap, double lumen or pseudoaneurysm
-> serial or complementary imaging resolves equivocal cases
```

#### Inferences

- Imaging protocols must look at both brain ischemia and arterial wall/lumen, because a normal-appearing lumen can miss wall hematoma.
- Ultrasound can be a triage or monitoring tool, but it is not enough when suspicion is high.
- Documentation should name both the suspected artery and the imaging sign supporting dissection.

#### Assumptions

- Imaging quality and local neuroradiology experience affect diagnostic confidence.
- Patient can undergo contrast or MRI unless contraindicated.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- V3 vertebral artery dissection can be difficult to diagnose by classic crescent sign because of vessel orientation and venous plexus signal.
- Multimodal source-image review may be needed; reconstructed angiographic images alone can be insufficient.
- This source does not provide a complete follow-up imaging schedule or treatment algorithm.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Suspect dissection in acute/subacute headache, neck pain and/or stroke symptoms, especially after even minor trauma, sports/physical activity, intense coughing/sneezing, peripartum Valsalva, connective tissue / vascular history, acute Horner syndrome, cranial/cervical neuropathy, pulsatile tinnitus or unexplained tooth pain.
- Do not dismiss dissection in older adults because classic pain/trigger features may be less frequent after age 60.
- Partial Horner syndrome with ptosis + miosis without anhidrosis is typical for internal carotid artery dissection.
- Vertebral artery dissection can present as posterior circulation ischemia, including lateral medullary infarction pattern.
- Intracranial dissection should be part of SAH differential, especially when vascular imaging does not support saccular aneurysm alone.
- Imaging confirmation should include head and neck vessels, not only brain parenchyma.

## Research-Useful Points

- Studies should separate extracranial vs intracranial dissection, carotid vs vertebral artery, local symptoms-only vs ischemia vs SAH, and imaging modality used for diagnosis.
- Reported incidence increases likely reflect more widespread CTA/MRA use and heightened awareness, not necessarily true biological incidence alone.
