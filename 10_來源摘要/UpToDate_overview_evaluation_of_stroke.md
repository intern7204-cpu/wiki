## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Stroke Evaluation from Presumptive Diagnosis to Directed Testing

#### One-Sentence Definition

Stroke evaluation is a staged diagnostic workflow: first stabilize and exclude hemorrhage / reperfusion-critical conditions, then form a mechanism hypothesis, then use directed neurovascular, cardiac, blood, and hemorrhage-focused testing to confirm the pathophysiology.

#### Known Facts

- Brain ischemia symptoms may be transient or persistent, but symptoms do not accurately indicate whether infarction is present.
- Symptom tempo alone does not identify ischemia cause.
- Treatment depends on accurately identifying the cause of symptoms.
- Initial evaluation includes classification, rapid stabilization, hemorrhage detection, reperfusion candidacy assessment, presumptive etiology formation, and confirmation with directed testing.
- Cerebrovascular disease mechanisms include intrinsic vessel abnormality, embolism, inadequate blood flow, and vessel rupture.
- Approximately 80 percent of strokes are ischemic cerebral infarction and 20 percent are brain hemorrhage in this source.
- The initial assessment goals are medical stability, reversal of contributing conditions, and movement toward pathophysiologic diagnosis.
- History and physical examination help distinguish mimics such as seizures, syncope, migraine, and hypoglycemia.
- Noncontrast CT is typically the first diagnostic study in suspected stroke because it is fast, widely available, and sensitive for acute hemorrhage.
- MRI is more sensitive than CT for early brain infarction; DWI and FLAIR sequences are useful early after symptom onset.
- In ischemia without infarction, CT and MRI may both be normal.
- A presumptive diagnosis can be made after history, physical examination, and CT/MRI, but confirmation requires more extensive testing.
- Brain imaging and comprehensive neurovascular evaluation should be obtained for most suspected acute ischemic stroke or TIA patients.
- Neurovascular imaging helps identify embolic or low-flow sources in ischemic stroke and aneurysm / vascular malformation in hemorrhagic stroke.
- Embolic stroke is favored by sudden maximal onset, large infarct/deficit, known cardiac or large-artery lesion, hemorrhagic infarct, multiple infarcts in different vascular territories, or rapid improvement.
- Extracranial and intracranial arterial sources should be studied when embolism is suspected.
- Suspected large-vessel atherothrombotic stroke requires both intracranial and extracranial vascular testing.
- Cardiac evaluation is essential in most patients with brain ischemia.
- All ischemic stroke patients should have cardiac monitoring for at least the first 24 hours after stroke onset to look for subclinical AF.
- For cryptogenic ischemic stroke or TIA without AF on ECG and 24-hour monitoring, the source suggests ambulatory cardiac monitoring for several weeks.
- All patients with suspected embolic stroke should have echocardiography.
- TTE is often the preferred initial test for most suspected cardiac/aortic embolic sources; TEE is preferred in selected scenarios such as young patients without known cardiovascular disease, high pretest cardiac embolic probability, AF when TEE would change management, prosthetic valves, or suspected aortic pathology.
- TEE better evaluates atria, atrial septal region, aorta, PFO, atrial septal aneurysm/defect, and left atrial appendage clot; TTE is better for left ventricular apical thrombus.
- Blood tests may include glucose, CBC/platelet count, PT/INR/aPTT, thrombin time/ecarin clotting time when direct anticoagulant use is suspected, and lipid profile.
- Widespread hypercoagulable testing is limited by low thrombophilia prevalence, altered test performance during acute ischemic events or anticoagulant use, and uncertain causal link between inherited thrombophilia and arterial stroke.
- Hypercoagulable testing is suggested in selected patients with personal/family systemic thrombosis, unexplained ischemic stroke/TIA after cardiac and vascular imaging especially in young patients, or clinical features suggesting SLE/APS.
- In intracerebral hemorrhage, CT defines size, location, intraventricular blood, mass effect, midline shift, herniation, and hydrocephalus.
- Typical hypertensive ICH in a severely hypertensive patient may need no further diagnostic testing.
- Every intracranial hemorrhage patient requires bleeding-disorder evaluation, especially if the cause is not immediately apparent.
- Lobar or atypical hemorrhage raises suspicion for cerebral amyloid angiopathy, tumor, or vascular malformation.
- Cocaine-associated ICH has relatively high incidence of underlying aneurysm or vascular malformation and requires vascular imaging.

#### Mechanism Chain

1. Acute neurologic symptoms may reflect ischemia, hemorrhage, or mimic.
2. The first diagnostic obligation is safety-critical triage: vital stability, reversible contributors, hemorrhage exclusion, and reperfusion candidacy.
3. History, physical examination, and early CT/MRI create a working hypothesis of stroke subtype and likely mechanism.
4. The working hypothesis determines which tests are high yield: vascular imaging, cardiac monitoring, echocardiography, selected blood tests, or hemorrhage etiology workup.
5. Confirmed mechanism then changes treatment: reperfusion, carotid intervention, anticoagulation, antiplatelet strategy, hemorrhage workup, or secondary prevention route.

#### Inferences

- A stroke evaluation note should distinguish "working mechanism" from "confirmed etiology."
- For rehabilitation teams, a new neurologic deficit should not be documented only as "stroke"; it should trigger the same mechanism-aware pathway: hemorrhage vs ischemia vs mimic, then directed testing.
- The diagnostic workflow is not a maximal checklist; it is a hypothesis-driven sequence.

#### Assumptions

- The patient is an adult with suspected acute stroke or TIA.
- CT/MRI and at least basic vascular/cardiac testing are available.
- Local stroke pathways may change test order depending on reperfusion eligibility and resource access.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This source is not a detailed neuroimaging guideline.
- It does not settle the optimal duration or modality of cardiac monitoring.
- It states rigorous evidence is lacking that anticoagulation of subclinical AF improves outcomes in patients initially diagnosed with cryptogenic stroke.
- Antiphospholipid antibody interpretation in ischemic stroke is conflicting.
- Pediatric, pregnancy-related, and rare genetic stroke causes require separate sources.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Do not wait for a full etiologic workup before hemorrhage/reperfusion triage.
- Do not stop at noncontrast CT if the question is ischemic mechanism; most suspected ischemic stroke/TIA patients need brain plus neurovascular evaluation.
- If the infarct is anterior circulation, focus vascular testing on extracranial/intracranial carotid and MCA/ACA branches.
- If the infarct is posterior circulation, focus on extracranial/intracranial vertebral arteries, basilar artery, and PCA territory.
- In suspected lacunar infarct, typical risk factors and characteristic imaging make further testing lower yield, but parent-artery occlusion can mimic lacunar stroke.
- Cardiac monitoring for at least 24 hours is a baseline ischemic stroke evaluation step; cryptogenic cases often need longer ambulatory monitoring.
- Hypercoagulable and antiphospholipid testing should be selected by pretest probability, not ordered routinely for every arterial stroke.

## Research-Useful Points

- Diagnostic studies should report how final mechanism was adjudicated rather than equating clinical pattern with etiology.
- Trials studying cryptogenic stroke should separate AF detection from proof that treating subclinical AF improves outcomes.
- Studies of hypercoagulable markers in arterial stroke need pretest-probability stratification and repeat/confirmatory testing logic.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with "CT negative means no ischemia."
- Conflicts with "symptom tempo alone identifies stroke cause."
- Conflicts with "AF or carotid stenosis automatically proves the mechanism."
- Conflicts with routine hypercoagulable panels for all ischemic strokes.
- Conflicts with treating lobar/atypical ICH as hypertensive hemorrhage without further etiologic consideration.
