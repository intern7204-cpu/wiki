# Source Summary: Nuuttila et al. 2026 - Low-Intensity Endurance Training Meta-analysis

## Source Type

- Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
- Journal context: *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports*, accepted 2026-01-02.
- Protocol registration: PROSPERO CRD42023469528.
- Raw source: `C:\原始資料\Scandinavian+Med+Sci+Sports+-+2026+-+Nuuttila+-+Effects+of+Low‐Intensity+Endurance+Training+on+Aerobic+Fitness+and+Risk\Scandinavian+Med+Sci+Sports+-+2026+-+Nuuttila+-+Effects+of+Low‐Intensity+Endurance+Training+on+Aerobic+Fitness+and+Risk.md`

## Reliability Level

- Tier 3: systematic review / meta-analysis.
- Evidence strength is strongest for aerobic fitness outcomes, especially VO2max and Pmax.
- Evidence strength is lower for cardiometabolic biomarkers because of heterogeneity, reporting limitations, and lower GRADE certainty for several outcomes.
- Best use: correcting claims about whether low-intensity endurance training (LIT) can improve fitness / cardiometabolic outcomes in healthy sedentary or untrained adults.

## One-Sentence Summary

Nuuttila et al. 2026 found that structured LIT improves aerobic fitness and several cardiometabolic risk markers in healthy working-age sedentary or untrained adults, with larger effects on fitness than biomarkers and with higher LIT intensity mainly linked to greater VO2max improvement.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Low-Intensity Endurance Training Evidence Boundary in Healthy Working-Age Adults

#### One-Sentence Definition

LIT is structured endurance training intended to remain below LT1 / VT1 or within conservative fixed-percent anchors, and in this source it is effective for improving aerobic fitness in healthy sedentary or untrained adults, while cardiometabolic effects are smaller and more heterogeneous.

#### Known Facts

- The review included randomized controlled trials in adults aged 18-65 years.
- Participants with diagnosed diseases, cardiometabolic medication use, pregnancy, BMI >35 kg/m2, nutritional interventions, ergogenic modalities, or pharmacologic agents were excluded.
- Training interventions had to last at least 3 weeks and use structured endurance training.
- LIT eligibility required training exclusively below LT1 / VT1, or if thresholds were not used, at <=60% VO2max, <=60% VO2 reserve, <=75% HRmax, or <=60% HRR.
- Literature search used PubMed and SPORTDiscus, English-language human studies, and publication dates before 2023-10-31.
- The review included 50 studies after screening 9702 records.
- There were 54 intervention groups and 50 control groups.
- Intervention groups included 824 participants and control groups included 708 participants.
- Participants were mainly sedentary or untrained; none of the included studies reported results among trained or competitive athletes.
- Mean intervention participant age was 35.5 +/- 12.5 years; control participant age was 33.2 +/- 11.8 years.
- Exercise intensity was reported by %HRmax in 21 studies, %VO2max in 20 studies, and %HRR in eight studies.
- Only one study used LT1 or VT1 to determine exercise intensity.
- Mean training frequency was 3.8 +/- 1.1 sessions/week.
- Mean session duration was 41 +/- 13 minutes.
- Mean intervention length was 12.2 +/- 5.7 weeks.
- Cycling and walking/running were the most common modes.
- Risk of bias: 86% of studies had some concerns and 14% had high risk of bias.
- LIT had a large effect on relative VO2max: ES 0.94, 95% CI 0.74-1.13.
- LIT had a large effect on absolute VO2max: ES 0.84, 95% CI 0.36-1.33.
- LIT had a large effect on Pmax: ES 1.09, 95% CI 0.86-1.31.
- LIT had a moderate effect on VT1: ES 0.74, 95% CI 0.26-1.22.
- LIT had small effects on total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure.
- Glucose effect was trivial.
- Training intensity within the LIT domain was associated with relative and absolute VO2max response in subgroup analyses.
- Meta-regression supported intensity dependence only for relative VO2max.
- Sex and baseline BMI did not significantly modify assessed outcomes.
- Baseline VO2max modified systolic blood pressure response.
- Age modified VO2max response.
- Sensitivity analyses excluding high-risk-of-bias studies or estimated maximum-value intensity prescription did not change outcome significance or magnitude.
- GRADE certainty was high for VO2max and Pmax, moderate for VT1, total cholesterol, LDL, and diastolic blood pressure, and low for HDL, glucose, triglycerides, and systolic blood pressure.

#### Mechanism Chain

1. Sedentary or untrained adults start with low endurance-training exposure and often lower aerobic fitness.
2. Structured LIT adds repeatable aerobic volume with relatively tolerable physiological strain.
3. Repeated low-intensity sessions increase central and peripheral aerobic demand without requiring high-intensity tolerance.
4. Aerobic fitness outcomes, especially VO2max and Pmax, improve with moderate-to-large pooled effects.
5. Cardiometabolic markers improve in smaller, less certain, and more heterogeneous ways.
6. Within LIT, higher intensity appears to generate a stronger VO2max stimulus.
7. For health-related biomarkers, added activity itself may matter more than exact intensity within the LIT domain.
8. If training is not progressed, many early adaptations may plateau.

#### Inferences

- In healthy sedentary or untrained working-age adults, "low intensity is too easy to work" is not supported by this source.
- If the target is VO2max, the upper end of LIT, roughly the area often marketed as Zone 2, may be more useful than very low LIT, but this source does not prove a universal Zone 2 threshold.
- If the target is cardiometabolic risk profile, LIT can be reasonable, but expected biomarker changes should be framed as small and heterogeneous.
- Clinical or coaching prescriptions should not rely only on fixed %HRmax / %VO2max when LT1 / VT1 is available.

#### Assumptions

- Fixed intensity anchors used in the included trials were intended to approximate exercise below LT1 / VT1 for most individuals.
- Structured LIT in sedentary / untrained adults can approximate a feasible public-health exercise entry point.
- VO2max / Pmax changes are more reliable indicators of training adaptation in this evidence set than cardiometabolic biomarkers.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Most studies did not use LT1 / VT1 for exercise prescription, so true physiological intensity domain may have varied.
- Some participants may have trained in the heavy-intensity domain despite being classified as LIT.
- Heterogeneity was notable across outcomes, especially cardiometabolic biomarkers.
- Many studies had incomplete adherence reporting; 24 studies did not report adherence at any level.
- None of the studies reported full training intensity distribution.
- Only five studies reported VT1, despite VO2max being widely reported.
- This source does not establish disease-specific contraindications, progression rules, or rehabilitation monitoring criteria.
- This source does not apply directly to trained or competitive athletes.

## Clinically Useful Points

- LIT is a defensible starting option for healthy sedentary or untrained adults when safety, tolerability, and adherence matter.
- For VO2max improvement, staying at the very bottom of the LIT domain may be less efficient than using the upper LIT range when safe and tolerable.
- For cardiometabolic biomarkers, the expected effect is smaller and should not be oversold.
- If available, LT1 / VT1 or validated ventilatory / lactate anchors are preferable to fixed percentage formulas.
- Lack of continued improvement after early gains should trigger progression review rather than declaring exercise failure.

## Research-Useful Points

- Future RCTs should prescribe LIT by LT1 / VT1 or verify whether fixed-percent anchors truly remain below threshold.
- Trials should report adherence, implemented intensity, training intensity distribution, background physical activity, and sedentary behavior.
- VT1 should be reported more consistently as a submaximal fitness outcome.
- Future work should separate VO2max response, threshold response, lipid response, blood pressure response, glucose response, and body mass effects.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with "LIT is ineffective for improving fitness in untrained adults."
- Conflicts with "only high intensity can produce cardiometabolic benefit."
- Conflicts with "any fixed Zone 2 / LIT percentage is physiologically equivalent across people."
- Conflicts with "Nuuttila 2026 proves Zone 2 is uniquely optimal"; the source supports LIT effectiveness and intensity-dependent VO2max response, not universal superiority.
