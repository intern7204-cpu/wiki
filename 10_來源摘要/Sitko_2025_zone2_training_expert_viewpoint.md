# Source Summary: Sitko et al. 2025 - What Is Zone 2 Training Expert Viewpoint

## Source Type

- Expert viewpoint / commentary.
- Expert panel: 14 applied sport scientists and professional coaches.
- Field focus: cycling and endurance training practice.
- Method: lead authors drafted a document around three questions, circulated it to the expert panel, categorized responses into intensity / training / adaptations, and revised until consensus.

## Reliability Level

- Tier 4: expert viewpoint / commentary.
- Useful for practical terminology, prescription language, and monitoring cues.
- Low reliability for proving clinical or performance superiority because it is not an intervention trial, systematic review, or meta-analysis.
- Best used as a practice-language supplement to [[Zone_2_Training_證據邊界]], not as stronger efficacy evidence.

## One-Sentence Summary

Sitko et al. describe Zone 2 training as a low-intensity endurance zone immediately below LT1 / VT1, usually monitored with lactate, HR, RPE, breathing/talk cues, and internal-load drift, while emphasizing that expected adaptations are not unique to Zone 2 and still require research confirmation.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Zone 2 Practical Definition and Monitoring

#### One-Sentence Definition

Zone 2 practical definition and monitoring refers to prescribing endurance training immediately below LT1 / VT1 and continuously checking whether internal load remains consistent with low-intensity steady-state work.

#### Known Facts

- The panel included 14 male experts from eight countries.
- The panel consisted of applied sport scientists and professional cycling coaches.
- The commentary aimed to improve communication among scientists, athletes, and coaches around Zone 2 training.
- The lead authors used PubMed and MEDLINE to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles on training intensity distribution and zones in cycling.
- The document was structured around three questions: how to define Zone 2, how to implement Zone 2 training, and what adaptations are expected.
- Twelve experts reported using a five-zone model for aerobic intensity distribution.
- Two experts did not use additional zones above traditional VO2max.
- In the five-zone model, LT1 or VT1 separated zones 1 and 2, and LT2 or VT2 / RCP separated zones 2 and 3.
- Experts located Zone 2 just below LT1 / VT1.
- Expected internal response profile included no change or only transient blood lactate increase around 1-2 mmol/L.
- Expected HR was approximately 70-80% HRmax or approximately 80-90% of LT1 HR.
- Expected Borg RPE was low, around 10 on the 6-20 scale.
- Expected power was approximately 75-80% of critical power.
- The table described I-2 as comfortable, CR10 RPE 2-3, Borg RPE 9-12, roughly 67-82% HRmax, conversation possible with some effort, and blood lactate around 1.0-2.0 mmol/L.
- Experts emphasized monitoring HR and RPE when sessions are prescribed by external load such as power.
- The source notes internal and external load can diverge during long sessions because of mental/physical fatigue, dehydration, glycogen depletion, cardiac drift, and related factors.
- Experts identified continuous, variable continuous, and interval-based methods.
- The preferred continuous method was long rides, ideally more than 2 hours, at the target intensity.
- If HR and RPE rise disproportionately during long sessions, the source recommends prioritizing HR/RPE zone maintenance even if external load must be reduced.
- Variable continuous sessions can intersperse Zone 2 with short Zone 1 periods; an example ratio was 5:1.
- Zone 2 can be used during active recovery between harder Zone 4 and 5 intervals.
- Expected adaptations included capillarization, mitochondrial enzymes in type I fibers, metabolic efficiency, modest critical power / VO2max increases, and LT1 / VT1 shifting toward LT2 / VT2.
- The source explicitly states research is needed to confirm the adaptation hypothesis.
- The source states expected adaptations are likely not unique to Zone 2 and can also be induced by slightly higher or lower intensities.

#### Mechanism Chain

1. Different systems use different intensity-zone demarcations, making "Zone 2" ambiguous.
2. Anchoring Zone 2 immediately below LT1 / VT1 links the coaching zone to a physiological transition point.
3. Internal-load markers such as HR, RPE, breathing, and lactate are used to check whether the work remains low-intensity and steady.
4. Long duration makes internal load drift possible even at fixed power.
5. Adjusting external load downward when HR/RPE drift upward helps preserve the intended internal-load target.
6. Repeated tolerable volume may support peripheral and central endurance adaptations, but the adaptations are not specific to Zone 2 and are not proven by this source.

#### Inferences

- In practice, Zone 2 should be prescribed as an internal-load target, not only a fixed wattage, pace, or percentage of HRmax.
- For clinical or rehabilitation use, the source supports using RPE / talk / HR drift monitoring language, but does not establish disease-specific safety or efficacy.
- The source can help translate LT1 / VT1 concepts into coaching instructions, but evidence hierarchy remains below systematic reviews and trials.

#### Assumptions

- LT1 / VT1 is available or can be approximated reliably enough for training prescription.
- Cycling-derived expert practice can inform other endurance sports after modality-specific modification.
- Long low-intensity sessions are feasible and relevant for the target athlete or client.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Expert panel was all male and cycling-focused.
- The process was not a formal Delphi with prespecified quantitative consensus thresholds.
- The source is not a systematic review.
- Expected adaptations were expert expectations, not direct findings from a controlled Zone 2 intervention.
- Clinical populations, older adults, medication effects, and rehabilitation safety rules were not the focus.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Practical Zone 2 can be monitored with HR, RPE, breathing/talk cues, lactate if available, and drift between external load and internal load.
- For a patient or athlete whose HR/RPE rises during a long session, reducing external load can be more faithful to the Zone 2 target than forcing the original wattage or pace.
- Talk and RPE cues are useful when laboratory thresholds are unavailable, but should be treated as proxies rather than exact physiology.

## Research-Useful Points

- Future Zone 2 studies should report how Zone 2 was verified: LT1, VT1, blood lactate, HR, RPE, power relative to critical power, or talk/breathing cues.
- Adaptation outcomes should separate capillarization, mitochondrial enzymes, metabolic efficiency, critical power, VO2max, durability, and internal-load response at fixed external load.
- Studies should not assume that adjacent zones cause categorically different adaptations.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with defining Zone 2 by fixed HR zones alone.
- Conflicts with treating external load as stable physiological intensity during long sessions.
- Conflicts with treating Zone 2 adaptations as unique.
- Conflicts with using this expert viewpoint as proof that Zone 2 is superior to higher-intensity training.
