# Source Summary: Posterior circulation cerebrovascular syndromes

原始檔：`原始資料/Posterior circulation cerebrovascular syndromes.md`

## Reliability Level

- Source tier: 6。
- 適合用途：posterior circulation stroke 的 vascular anatomy、topographic syndrome、bedside localization、diagnostic pitfall and acute vestibular syndrome framing。

## One-Sentence Summary

Posterior circulation ischemia 涉及 vertebral、basilar and posterior cerebral artery territories，臨床常以 vertigo/dizziness、ataxia、ocular motor signs、bulbar weakness、altered alertness/behavior/memory、visual loss 或 PCA cortical syndrome 表現，且 early CT / DWI MRI 可漏診，因此需要把 vascular territory、topographic signs and HINTS 適用邊界一起看。

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Posterior circulation stroke 臨床定位與診斷陷阱

#### One-Sentence Definition

Posterior circulation stroke 是 vertebrobasilar arterial territory 的 ischemic syndrome，診斷重點不是單一症狀，而是把 vascular anatomy、brainstem/cerebellar/PCA signs、acute timing and imaging limitations 合併判讀。

#### Known Facts

- Posterior circulation structures account for about 20 percent of ischemic brain events in this source.
- Large posterior circulation arteries include innominate and subclavian arteries, extracranial vertebral arteries, intracranial vertebral arteries, basilar artery and PCAs.
- Proximal ECVA atherosclerotic disease is a common posterior circulation occlusive lesion site.
- With proximal ECVA stenosis, artery-to-artery embolism is more frequent than low-flow hemodynamic insufficiency as the mechanism of intracranial posterior circulation ischemia.
- Basilar artery occlusive disease most often presents as pontine ischemia, with motor, bulbar and oculomotor abnormalities.
- Top of the basilar syndrome involves rostral basilar territory, affecting midbrain, thalami, temporal and occipital PCA territories.
- PCA infarction most often presents with hemianopia; lateral thalamic infarction is a major cause of somatosensory findings in PCA territory ischemia.
- CT is less sensitive than DWI MRI for acute ischemic stroke, especially posterior fossa stroke; DWI MRI can still be false negative in small acute posterior circulation infarcts.

#### Mechanism Chain

Posterior circulation arterial lesion  
-> vertebral / basilar / PCA branch flow or embolic pathway  
-> brainstem, cerebellar, thalamic, occipital or medial temporal ischemia  
-> ocular motor, bulbar, respiratory/autonomic, motor, sensory, vestibulocerebellar, visual or cognitive-behavioral findings  
-> clinical suspicion may exceed early imaging sensitivity  
-> directed vascular imaging and repeat/advanced evaluation may be needed when suspicion remains high.

#### Inferences

- A "dizzy stroke" cannot be decided from dizziness alone; continuous acute vestibular syndrome, ocular motor pattern, gait/limb ataxia, new hearing loss, bulbar signs, sensory pattern and posterior fossa imaging limitations must be integrated.
- In rehabilitation or ward settings, new dysphagia, unexplained ataxia, diplopia, altered alertness or posterior headache/neck pain should not be assumed to be baseline fluctuation without stroke reconsideration.

#### Assumptions

- Adult suspected ischemic stroke setting.
- Examiner can perform focused neuro-ophthalmologic and cerebellar/bulbar examination.
- CT/MRI and vascular imaging are available for confirmation.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Several topographic syndromes overlap clinically.
- Anatomic variants such as fetal PCA and artery of Percheron can alter expected vascular-territory logic.
- Vertebral artery hypoplasia association with posterior circulation ischemic stroke is suggested by some data but not established in this source.
- HINTS is not useful for momentary positional vertigo or TIA when the patient is not dizzy at examination.

### Concept: HINTS in acute vestibular syndrome

#### One-Sentence Definition

HINTS is a three-part bedside oculomotor exam for continuous acute vertigo/dizziness that uses head impulse, nystagmus direction and skew deviation to separate likely central brainstem/cerebellar ischemia from peripheral vestibular disease.

#### Known Facts

- HINTS is most useful in continuous acute vertigo or dizziness.
- It is not useful for momentary position-related transient vertigo or TIA when the patient is not dizzy during examination.
- Normal head impulse bilaterally, direction-changing nystagmus or skew deviation suggests a brainstem or cerebellar lesion.
- Abnormal unilateral head impulse, unidirectional horizontal-torsional nystagmus and absent skew together suggest a peripheral lesion.
- The source states HINTS is more sensitive than brain MRI within the first two days after symptom onset for acute stroke diagnosis in this context.
- Inner ear infarction from internal auditory artery / AICA branch occlusion can mimic peripheral vestibular HINTS pattern; new ipsilateral hearing loss may be the clue.

#### Mechanism Chain

Vestibular symptom  
-> determine whether syndrome is continuous acute vestibular syndrome  
-> examine vestibulo-ocular reflex, nystagmus direction and ocular alignment  
-> central pattern raises concern for brainstem/cerebellar ischemia  
-> negative early imaging does not fully exclude posterior circulation stroke.

#### Inferences

- HINTS should be documented as a syndrome-specific exam, not a generic dizziness screen.
- A normal early MRI should not override persistent central HINTS signs or dangerous posterior circulation features.

#### Assumptions

- The examiner is trained enough to perform and interpret HINTS.
- The patient is currently symptomatic with continuous vertigo/dizziness.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- HINTS accuracy depends on training background and stroke location.
- AICA / internal auditory artery infarction can produce new hearing loss and a peripheral-appearing vestibular pattern.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Posterior circulation ischemia should be suspected with rapid/sudden neurologic symptoms plus altered alertness/behavior/memory, visual loss, ocular motor or pupillary abnormality, bulbar weakness, somatosensory loss, dizziness/vertigo, limb ataxia or gait ataxia.
- Lateral medullary infarction commonly combines vestibulocerebellar symptoms, crossed pain/temperature sensory pattern, dysphagia/hoarseness, Horner syndrome and possible respiratory/autonomic dysfunction.
- PICA territory cerebellar infarction can mimic peripheral vestibulopathy; large PICA infarcts can evolve with edema, mass effect, worsening headache/vomiting and decreased consciousness after the first day.
- AICA territory infarction may include vertigo, unsteady gait, dysarthria, ipsilateral LMN facial weakness/numbness, tinnitus and early ipsilateral hearing loss.
- Basilar occlusive disease often produces bilateral but asymmetric motor findings, bulbar weakness, aspiration risk and oculomotor abnormalities; locked-in syndrome is the extreme voluntary motor-loss phenotype with retained consciousness.
- PCA infarcts can produce hemianopia, hemisensory loss, alexia without agraphia, Gerstmann syndrome, amnesia, associative visual agnosia, prosopagnosia, spatial disorientation or visual neglect depending on side and territory.

## Research-Useful Points

- Posterior circulation stroke studies should separate territory, mechanism, imaging sensitivity, examiner expertise for HINTS and timing from onset.
- Diagnostic outcome studies should not treat early negative DWI as absolute non-stroke when posterior circulation symptoms persist.
- Studies of dizziness/vertigo need to separate continuous acute vestibular syndrome from transient positional vertigo and resolved TIA symptoms.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with "CT or early MRI negative excludes posterior circulation stroke."
- Conflicts with "dizziness or vertigo alone is enough to diagnose vertebrobasilar ischemia."
- Conflicts with "all acute vertigo with peripheral-appearing HINTS is benign" because AICA / internal auditory artery infarction can mimic peripheral vestibular disease.
- Conflicts with "subclavian steal physiology commonly causes stroke" because the source states neurologic symptoms are uncommon unless there is accompanying carotid disease and strokes are seldom caused by subclavian/innominate disease.
- Conflicts with "proximal ECVA disease mainly causes low-flow stroke" because artery-to-artery embolism is described as more frequent.
