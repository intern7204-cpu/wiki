## One-Sentence Summary

Clinical diagnosis of stroke subtype uses time course, associated symptoms, risk factors, and physical / neurologic examination to estimate ischemic thrombotic, embolic, hypoperfusion, ICH, or SAH mechanism, but the presumptive diagnosis requires brain and vascular imaging confirmation.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Stroke Subtype Clinical Diagnosis

#### One-Sentence Definition

Stroke subtype clinical diagnosis is a probabilistic bedside process that maps symptom onset pattern, neurologic course, associated symptoms, risk factors, and exam clues onto ischemic versus hemorrhagic stroke mechanisms before imaging confirmation.

#### Known Facts

- Stroke subtypes in this source include ICH, SAH, and brain ischemia due to thrombosis, embolism, or systemic hypoperfusion.
- Thrombosis means local in situ arterial obstruction, usually from arterial wall disease such as atherosclerosis, dissection, or fibromuscular dysplasia.
- Thrombotic strokes may be large vessel or small vessel disease.
- Thrombosis-related neurologic symptoms often fluctuate, remit, or progress in a stepwise / stuttering fashion.
- Embolism means debris from another site blocks arterial access to a brain region.
- Embolic stroke may originate from the heart, aorta, or large vessels.
- Embolic symptoms are usually abrupt and maximal at onset.
- Multiple affected vascular territories favor a cardiac or aortic embolic source.
- Systemic hypoperfusion is a global circulatory problem and usually causes diffuse, nonfocal brain dysfunction, sometimes bilateral signs.
- ICH usually arises from arterioles or small arteries, bleeds directly into brain tissue, and neurologic symptoms usually increase gradually over minutes or hours.
- SAH from aneurysm rupture releases blood into CSF under arterial pressure, raises intracranial pressure rapidly, and usually begins abruptly.
- Severe widespread headache at onset is a characteristic SAH symptom.
- Onset headache is more common in SAH than ICH, and onset headache plus vomiting is uncommon in ischemic stroke.
- The most important historical item for differentiating stroke subtypes is the pace, course, and clearing of symptoms and signs.
- It can be useful to ask what the patient could still do after first symptoms, such as walking, talking, using a phone, or using the hand.
- Neurologic deterioration during the acute phase, 48 to 72 hours from ischemic stroke onset, occurred in 256 of 1964 patients in the cited prospective study.
- In that study, deterioration was mainly related to progressive infarction, increased intracranial pressure, recurrent cerebral ischemia, and secondary parenchymal hemorrhage.
- Independent predictors of neurologic deterioration included internal carotid artery occlusion, brainstem infarction, MCA M1 occlusion, territorial infarction, and diabetes mellitus.
- Silent brain infarcts are infarcts identified only by neuroimaging without clinical stroke / TIA history, but the source states covert brain infarcts is a more appropriate term.
- Covert brain infarcts may be associated with cognitive deficits and increased future stroke / dementia risk.
- Ecology and risk factors raise probabilities but cannot make a firm diagnosis by themselves.
- Heart disease, especially atrial fibrillation, valvular disease, recent MI, and endocarditis, increases the probability of embolic stroke.
- Atrial fibrillation is the most prominent cardiac risk factor and causes nearly half of cardioembolic strokes in this source.
- Hypertension is the most common and most important stroke risk factor and is relevant to both thrombotic stroke and ICH.
- Severe uncontrolled hypertension is a strong risk factor for ICH.
- Diabetes increases the likelihood of large and small artery occlusive disease and ischemic stroke.
- Prior TIA in the same territory strongly favors a local vascular lesion / thrombosis; attacks in multiple vascular territories suggest embolism from the heart or aorta.
- TIAs are not a feature of brain hemorrhage.
- Hemorrhages can be precipitated by sex or physical activity; trauma before stroke suggests traumatic dissection, arterial occlusion, or traumatic brain hemorrhage.
- Fever raises suspicion for endocarditis and embolic stroke.
- Severe headache at onset favors SAH; headache developing after symptom onset with worsening neurologic signs, decreased consciousness, and vomiting favors ICH.
- Vomiting is common in ICH, SAH, and posterior circulation large artery ischemia.
- Acute seizures are most often seen in lobar ICH or brain embolism and are less common in acute thrombosis.
- In one population-based study cited by this source, seizure within 24 hours occurred in 10 percent of SAH, 8 percent of ICH, and 3 percent of ischemic stroke.
- Reduced alertness favors hemorrhage, but can also occur with large thrombotic / embolic strokes, posterior circulation large artery strokes, pontine tegmental ischemia, or large hemispheric infarct edema.
- Absent pulses or neck bruit may suggest atherosclerotic occlusive disease / thrombosis.
- Atrial fibrillation, murmur, and cardiac enlargement favor cardiac-origin embolism.
- Optic fundus embolic material may support embolic mechanism; subhyaloid hemorrhage suggests suddenly developing brain or SAH.
- Pure motor stroke favors penetrating artery thrombotic stroke or small ICH.
- Language abnormality suggests anterior circulation disease.
- Vertigo, staggering, diplopia, deafness, crossed signs, bilateral motor / sensory signs, and hemianopsia suggest posterior circulation involvement.
- Sudden impaired consciousness without focal neurologic signs is characteristic of SAH.
- Neuroimaging is necessary to identify hemorrhage, assess brain injury, and identify the vascular lesion responsible for ischemic deficit.
- NT-proBNP and D-dimer may have the strongest biomarker data for mechanism differentiation, but no biomarkers have sufficient sensitivity or specificity for routine clinical use.

#### Mechanism Chain

1. Different stroke mechanisms injure brain tissue through different immediate pathophysiology.
2. Embolic occlusion abruptly blocks a recipient artery, so deficits are usually sudden and maximal at onset.
3. Local thrombosis evolves around an unstable arterial lesion, so symptoms may fluctuate or progress in a stuttering pattern.
4. Penetrating artery occlusion often evolves over hours to a few days and may produce lacunar syndromes such as pure motor stroke.
5. ICH creates an expanding hematoma, so deficits often progress over minutes to hours and reduced alertness / vomiting are more common.
6. SAH abruptly raises intracranial pressure and irritates meninges, so thunderclap severe headache and sudden activity cessation are central clues.
7. Risk factors and exam findings shift probabilities but overlap across mechanisms.
8. Therefore clinical subtype diagnosis remains presumptive until imaging confirms hemorrhage, infarction pattern, and vascular lesion.

#### Inferences

- In acute and rehabilitation settings, a new neurologic change should not be labelled simply as "stroke recurrence" without considering subtype time course and hemorrhagic conversion.
- Bedside subtype clues are useful because acute BP, antithrombotic, reperfusion, mobilization, and neurosurgical logic differ sharply between ischemic stroke, ICH, and SAH.
- A probabilistic subtype impression should be documented as "suspected" or "favored" until imaging confirms the mechanism.
- Covert brain infarcts should be treated as clinically meaningful risk markers, not ignored as harmless incidental lesions.

#### Assumptions

- The clinician can obtain a reliable witness history or reconstruct symptom progression from functionally concrete questions.
- Brain and vascular imaging are available to confirm or revise the bedside impression.
- The patient is an adult; pediatric stroke patterns require separate sources.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Many risk factors, such as hypertension and atherosclerosis, predispose to more than one stroke subtype.
- Some ICH can present abruptly and some ischemic strokes can deteriorate later, so course is not perfectly specific.
- Neurologic signs localize vascular territory better than they identify pathologic mechanism.
- Biomarkers are not adequate for routine subtype diagnosis.
- This topic does not fully cover detailed stroke etiology classification, acute reperfusion therapy, ICH management, SAH management, or secondary prevention.

## Clinically Useful Points

- First ask about onset and evolution, not only current deficit.
- Use functional reconstruction: could the patient walk, talk, use the phone, or use the hand after the first symptom?
- Abrupt maximal deficit favors embolism; stuttering deficits favor thrombosis; gradual progressive minutes-to-hours deficit favors ICH; instant thunderclap headache favors SAH.
- Same-territory prior TIAs favor local thrombosis; multi-territory attacks favor embolic source.
- AF and cardiac findings favor embolism, but AF does not exclude other mechanisms.
- Pure motor syndrome supports lacunar / penetrating artery disease or small ICH.
- Posterior circulation symptoms can include vertigo, diplopia, deafness, crossed signs, bilateral signs, hemianopsia, vomiting, and impaired consciousness.
- Imaging confirmation is mandatory before definitive subtype-based treatment.

## Research-Useful Points

- Studies of stroke subtype diagnosis should separate clinical suspicion, brain imaging, vascular imaging, and final etiologic classification.
- Covert brain infarct studies need careful history because some "silent" lesions may be clinically unrecognized rather than truly asymptomatic.
- Biomarker panels should be judged against sensitivity / specificity high enough to change acute decisions, not just statistical association.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with "history and exam are enough to diagnose stroke subtype."
- Conflicts with "AF automatically proves cardioembolic stroke."
- Conflicts with "headache and vomiting mean ischemic stroke is unlikely in all cases"; posterior circulation ischemia can also cause vomiting.
- Conflicts with "silent brain infarcts are clinically irrelevant."
- Conflicts with "blood biomarkers can routinely classify stroke mechanism."
