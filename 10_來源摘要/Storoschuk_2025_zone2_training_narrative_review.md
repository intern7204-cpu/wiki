# Source Summary: Storoschuk et al. 2025 - Much Ado About Zone 2

## Source Type

- Narrative review article.
- Journal context: *Sports Medicine* 2025; accepted 2025-04-07, published online 2025-06-25.
- Raw source: `C:\原始資料\Much-Ado-About-Zone-2-A-Narrative-Review-Assessing-the-Efficacy-of-Zone-2-Training-for-Improving\Much-Ado-About-Zone-2-A-Narrative-Review-Assessing-the-Efficacy-of-Zone-2-Training-for-Improving.md`

## Reliability Level

- Tier 4: narrative review / high-quality review context.
- Evidence strength: moderate for conceptual critique and evidence mapping; lower than systematic review / meta-analysis because the search was not systematic.
- Best use: correcting overclaiming around Zone 2 training, not generating a rigid exercise prescription algorithm.

## One-Sentence Summary

Storoschuk et al. 2025 argue that Zone 2 training can be useful, especially for fatty acid oxidative adaptations in untrained populations, but available evidence does not support the popular claim that Zone 2 is uniquely optimal for mitochondrial capacity, fatty acid oxidative capacity, or cardiorespiratory fitness in the general population.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Zone 2 Training Evidence Boundary

#### One-Sentence Definition

Zone 2 training is low- to moderate-intensity exercise commonly placed below LT1 / around Fatmax / at conversational intensity, but this source argues that its public-media status as the optimal mitochondrial and metabolic-health intensity exceeds the evidence.

#### Known Facts

- The review defines the target population as the general population, meaning non-endurance-trained individuals who are insufficiently active or meeting physical activity guidelines.
- The review did not use a systematic literature search.
- Articles were obtained through database searches, reference lists, relevant systematic reviews, social-media-shared articles, and literature known to the authors.
- Zone 2 was operationalized as exercise below LT1 or showing physiological responses consistent with the moderate-intensity domain.
- The review used criteria such as BLa < 2.0 mmol/L, below ventilatory threshold 1, below Fatmax, or <45% VO2max when judging whether studies approximated Zone 2 exercise.
- Popular Zone 2 claims often combine Fatmax, BLa just below LT1 around 1.7-2.0 mmol/L, and Talk Test conversational intensity.
- The authors note that LT1 / Zone 2 boundaries vary widely by fitness and training status.
- In the review's figure example, LT1 ranged from 23% to 57% of WRpeak across three individuals.
- The authors state that elite endurance athletes often perform large volumes of low-intensity training but also perform high-intensity training and train at much higher total volumes than public-health targets.
- Zone 2 exercise produced small and inconsistent activation of mitochondrial biogenic signaling.
- Few studies explicitly investigated Zone 2 training effects on mitochondrial outcomes.
- The available evidence on Zone 2 and mitochondrial capacity was mixed.
- The review cites meta-analytic evidence suggesting exercise below 60% maximum work rate is not expected to improve mitochondrial content or mitochondrial respiratory capacity.
- The authors argue that exercise above Zone 2 may be superior for inducing mitochondrial adaptations.
- Zone 2 training appears capable of increasing fatty acid oxidative capacity, likely mainly in sedentary / untrained populations.
- Only one cited study measured FAO after confirmed Zone 2 training with BLa < 2.0 mmol/L, showing increased FATmax and MFO after 1 year in previously sedentary adults.
- FATmax-anchored training studies cannot always be interpreted as Zone 2 evidence because FATmax can occur above Zone 2 in sedentary populations.
- Evidence that Zone 2 is superior to higher exercise intensities for FAO is not convincing.
- For CRF, comparisons show no difference, higher-intensity superiority, or benefit only with higher intensities depending on population and study.
- The authors argue that replacing higher-intensity exercise with Zone 2 may reduce potential CRF and cardiometabolic benefits when training time is limited.

#### Mechanism Chain

1. Zone 2 is placed within the moderate-intensity domain, usually below LT1 / GET.
2. This domain produces relatively low energetic stress, lower PCr perturbation, lower glycolytic flux, and minimal lactate accumulation.
3. Lower energetic stress can make Zone 2 tolerable and sustainable, but it also produces weaker or inconsistent AMPK / PGC-1alpha and mitochondrial biogenic signaling.
4. Long-duration Zone 2 may still produce glycogen depletion and some adaptation signals, but this often requires high volume.
5. Higher intensities above Zone 2 produce larger energy perturbation, greater mitochondrial signaling, and stronger CRF stimulus for a fixed time budget.
6. Zone 2 may still improve FAO through mechanisms such as substrate-use adaptation, capillary / lipid-transport changes, and improved use of non-plasma fatty acids, especially in untrained or obese populations.
7. Therefore Zone 2 is plausible as one useful training tool, but not as a uniquely optimal or exclusive intensity.

#### Inferences

- The clinical / public-health question should not be "Zone 2 or high intensity" but whether the individual's total prescription includes enough safe intensity to improve the target outcome.
- Zone 2 may be especially useful when adherence, recovery, orthopedic tolerance, or high-volume base work matters.
- For time-limited improvement of CRF or mitochondrial respiratory capacity, exclusively prioritizing Zone 2 is probably a weak strategy.
- Public claims that a person must avoid exceeding Zone 2 to obtain mitochondrial benefits are not supported by this source.

#### Assumptions

- LT1 / GET and moderate-intensity domain are acceptable approximations for the popular Zone 2 construct.
- Evidence from adjacent low-intensity, FATmax, moderate-intensity, and below-threshold exercise studies can inform Zone 2 claims, despite imperfect alignment.
- General-population recommendations should weigh CRF and cardiometabolic outcomes more heavily than a single mechanistic marker.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- The review was not systematic and did not use formal risk-of-bias or meta-analytic synthesis.
- Few studies directly test Zone 2 as popularly defined.
- Zone 2 definitions vary across lactate, ventilatory, FATmax, talk-test, and zone-based coaching models.
- Whether Zone 2 improves FAO across different training status, sex, metabolic disease, and medication contexts remains uncertain.
- The optimal intensity for FAO improvement remains unclear.
- The review does not provide disease-specific contraindications or progression algorithms.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Do not tell the general public that Zone 2 is uniquely optimal for mitochondria or cardiometabolic health.
- Use Zone 2 as one option within FITT, not as a substitute for all higher-intensity work.
- In deconditioned, obese, metabolic syndrome, or rehabilitation contexts, Zone 2-like work may be useful for tolerability and FAO-related goals, but progression should still follow safety, symptoms, and objective response.
- If the target is CRF improvement, the prescription likely needs either higher intensity, longer duration, or both.

## Research-Useful Points

- Future studies should directly prescribe and verify Zone 2 using clear physiological anchors such as LT1 / GET, BLa, Fatmax, or ventilatory measures.
- Studies should separate mitochondrial content, mitochondrial respiration, biogenic signaling, FAO, CRF, and health outcomes rather than treating them as interchangeable.
- Public-media claims need to be separated from evidence generated in elite endurance athletes.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with "Zone 2 is the best intensity for mitochondrial function."
- Conflicts with "higher intensity cancels Zone 2 mitochondrial benefits."
- Conflicts with "elite endurance athletes do a lot of low-intensity work, therefore non-athletes should prioritize only Zone 2."
- Conflicts with "Fatmax-anchored training always equals Zone 2."
