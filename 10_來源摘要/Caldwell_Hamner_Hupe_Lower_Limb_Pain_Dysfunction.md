## One-Sentence Summary

Lower limb pain should be approached as a sequential history-and-exam problem: localize symptoms by region, preserve important referred and mimic diagnoses, identify high-risk bone / tendon / pediatric / intraarticular conditions, and use imaging or referral only when it answers a defined clinical question.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Lower Limb Pain 分區定位與高風險分流

#### One-Sentence Definition

Lower limb pain evaluation begins by mapping symptoms to hip / groin, thigh / knee, lower leg, ankle, or foot regions, then immediately separating routine overuse patterns from high-risk diagnoses such as pediatric hip pathology, femoral neck stress injury, Achilles rupture, osteochondral lesion, and high-risk stress fracture sites.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter is organized around history and clinical examination rather than exhaustive disease listing.
- Hip pain can present as groin, buttock, lateral hip / greater trochanteric pain, low back pain, or thigh pain.
- Hip pain may be divided into anterior, lateral, posterior, and medial regions.
- Anterior hip pain includes avascular necrosis, dislocation, labral pathology, femoroacetabular impingement, hip OA, and common pediatric hip presentations.
- Posterior hip pain should preserve spine, hamstring, deep gluteal, and pelvic referral diagnoses.
- Lateral hip pain should preserve tendinopathy, spine referral, and GTPS.
- Medial hip / groin pain can include sports groin / athletic pubalgia.
- The chapter explicitly warns that muscle, bone, nerve, and tendon pain must be considered in all hip regions, especially stress fractures and fractures in athletes.
- AVNFH presents with anterior hip / groin pain worsened by weight bearing; early radiographs may be normal and MRI / CT can detect earlier disease.
- LCPD is idiopathic osteonecrosis of the femoral epiphysis in children, can deform the femoral head, and may lead to premature OA.
- SCFE is the most common adolescent hip pathology, can present as hip / groin pain, knee pain, limp, or painless external-rotation gait, and should be treated as an orthopedic emergency.
- GTPS is a regional pain syndrome; bursal inflammation has a limited role and gluteal tendinopathy, ITB thickening, and abductor tendon pathology are more frequent contributors.
- GTPS can mimic lumbar pathology and may radiate to the groin, low back, lateral thigh, or knee.
- PFP is common in active populations and relates to patellar tracking in the trochlea with quadriceps and hip abductor dysfunction.
- Quadriceps or patellar tendon rupture clues include sudden mechanism, palpable defect, and inability to extend the knee; the chapter recommends immediate immobilization, avoidance of weight bearing, and surgical referral.
- Femoral bone stress injury may present as vague activity-related pain mimicking muscle strain; radiographs can be negative early and MRI / CT / bone scan detect earlier injury.
- Femoral neck tension-side stress injury is high risk and may require surgical referral or intervention.
- MTSS is described as part of a spectrum that may progress toward stress fracture.
- Tibial stress fracture presents with focal activity- and weight-bearing-related pain; MRI is preferred when suspicion remains.
- The middle anterior tibial cortex with a “dreaded black line” is high risk for nonunion or complete fracture.
- Achilles rupture can present as feeling “kicked in the leg,” a pop, immediate pain, swelling, and possibly palpable defect; Thompson test is important because active plantarflexion can persist despite rupture.
- Lateral ankle sprains are most common, usually initially involving the ATFL, and grade 1-2 injuries are commonly managed with early mobilization, balance / proprioception, and strengthening.
- Persistent or recurrent ankle symptoms should raise concern for chronic instability, peroneal injury, bony injury, syndesmotic injury, or osteochondral lesion.
- Osteochondral lesion of the talus can cause pain, swelling, locking, and stiffness; radiographs may miss many lesions and advanced imaging may be needed.
- Foot pain conditions discussed include cuboid subluxation, metatarsal stress fracture, plantar fasciitis, metatarsalgia, and Morton neuroma.
- High-risk stress fracture sites listed by the chapter include femoral neck tension side, middle anterior tibia, navicular bone, fifth metatarsal Jones fracture, and talar dome fracture.
- The chapter concludes that many lower-extremity pain problems mimic other conditions, imaging can confirm clinical diagnosis / severity / healing, most injuries are conservative, and clinicians must recognize problems needing surgical consultation.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Lower limb pain complaint
-> regional localization: hip/groin, thigh/knee, lower leg, ankle, foot
-> history and exam identify load pattern, referral pattern, pediatric risk, bone-health risk, tendon rupture signs, neurovascular signs, instability, locking, and weight-bearing tolerance
-> differential separates common overuse or soft-tissue patterns from high-risk bone, tendon, pediatric, intraarticular, or neurovascular diagnoses
-> imaging answers a defined question when exam/course suggests fracture, stress injury, osteochondral lesion, tendon tear, or diagnosis mismatch
-> urgent referral is used for orthopedic emergencies, rupture, high-risk stress fracture, unstable lesion, or failed conservative course
```

#### Inferences

- A lower limb pain note should explicitly show the region map and high-risk screen before naming a single diagnosis.
- In athletes and runners, progressive focal bone pain should be treated differently from diffuse soft-tissue soreness because high-risk stress fracture sites have different consequences.
- Persistent symptoms after a common label such as ankle sprain, plantar fasciitis, PFP, or GTPS should trigger re-checking for mimic diagnoses rather than simply repeating the same treatment.
- Pediatric hip pain or knee pain can be hip-origin disease and should not be dismissed as local knee overuse.

#### Assumptions

- The clinician has enough history and physical examination data to define the dominant pain region and weight-bearing / activity pattern.
- Imaging, when used, is chosen to answer a specific diagnostic or management question.
- Referral access is available for suspected orthopedic emergency, high-risk stress fracture, rupture, unstable intraarticular lesion, or refractory course.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This chapter is not a formal guideline and does not replace Ottawa ankle rules, pediatric hip protocols, sports-specific return-to-play criteria, or updated condition-specific guidelines.
- The chapter covers many diagnoses; it is useful for differential structure but does not give full rehabilitation protocols for each condition.
- Some injection, biologic, shockwave, bracing, supplement, or regenerative-medicine statements require condition-specific evidence updates before being used as standard care.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Start lower limb pain evaluation with symptom region, onset, load change, weight-bearing tolerance, referral pattern, neurologic / vascular symptoms, systemic context, and prior injury.
- Treat pediatric limp, hip / groin pain, or unexplained knee pain as possible hip pathology until excluded.
- For lateral hip pain, consider GTPS as gluteal tendon / abductor / ITB-region pathology rather than defaulting to isolated bursitis.
- For anterior knee pain, distinguish PFP from extensor mechanism rupture, dislocation, osteochondritis dissecans, fat-pad, plica, bursitis, or pediatric traction apophysitis.
- For lower-leg pain, separate diffuse MTSS-like pain from focal tibial stress injury and remember anterior tibial cortex risk.
- For Achilles-region pain, do Thompson test and inspect for rupture because active plantarflexion alone can mislead.
- For ankle sprain, early ROM and progressive proprioception / strength are appropriate for many lateral sprains, but persistent pain / locking / swelling / recurrent instability deserves reassessment.
- Keep the high-risk stress fracture list visible: femoral neck tension side, middle anterior tibia, navicular, Jones fracture, and talar dome.

## Research-Useful Points

- Studies and clinical notes should separate region-based syndromes from final tissue diagnoses.
- Outcomes should distinguish pain reduction, tissue healing, recurrence, function, return to sport, and participation.
- Future synthesis should compare textbook differential structure against updated guideline-level evidence for pediatric hip pathology, stress fractures, ankle sprain, Achilles rupture, PFP, GTPS, and foot pain.
