# Source Summary: Common Neck Problems

- Textbook chapter / clinical rehabilitation chapter.
- Authors listed in source: Michael J. DePalma and Shoji Ishigami.
- Single-source ingest only.

## Reliability Level

- Procedure-specific claims for cervical injections, RFA, and surgery should be checked against current specialty guidelines before use.

## One-Sentence Summary

Neck pain management begins by separating axial cervical pain, somatic referred pain, radicular pain, radiculopathy, and myelopathy because these patterns require different imaging, EDX, injection, rehabilitation, and surgical thresholds.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Axial vs radicular vs somatic referred neck pain

#### One-Sentence Definition

Neck-related pain is not one category: axial pain stays in the neck / interscapular corridor, somatic referred pain spreads through convergence without neural dysfunction, and radicular pain follows an irritated nerve root.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter states that distinguishing cervical axial pain from upper limb radicular pain is essential.
- Axial neck pain is described in the corridor from inferior occiput to superior interscapular region, often midline or paramidline.
- Radicular pain extends into the shoulder girdle or distal upper limb.
- Nonneural cervical structures such as disc, facet, PLL, and muscles can refer pain to head or upper limb through convergence.
- Upper limb symptoms therefore do not automatically equal radiculopathy.

#### Inferences

- Imaging or treatment chosen for radiculopathy may be inappropriate for somatic referred pain.
- A pain distribution map alone is insufficient without neurological exam and provocation / relief pattern.

### Concept: Cervical radicular pain and radiculopathy

#### One-Sentence Definition

Cervical radicular pain is nerve-root pain, while cervical radiculopathy requires neurophysiologic dysfunction such as myotomal weakness, sensory change, paresthesia, or depressed reflex.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter differentiates radicular pain from radiculopathy.
- Common causes include cervical intervertebral disc herniation and spondylosis.
- Inflammation may trigger symptoms; compression alone does not necessarily cause pain.
- MRI is the imaging gold standard, but asymptomatic abnormalities are common and must be clinically correlated.
- Spurling test is described as highly specific but not sensitive.
- EDX helps distinguish root disease from plexopathy and peripheral entrapment.
- Progressive neurologic deficit is a definitive surgical indication in the chapter.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Cervical disc / spondylotic change or inflammation
→ nerve root irritation or dysfunction
→ radicular pain ± myotomal / reflex / sensory findings
→ clinical correlation with MRI / EDX
→ conservative care, injection, or surgery depending on severity and progression
```

#### Inferences

- A positive MRI without congruent symptoms and exam should not be treated as the sole diagnosis.
- Severe weakness inconsistent with one root should trigger broader differential such as myelopathy, plexopathy, ALS, or multilevel disease.

### Concept: Cervical myelopathy red flags

#### One-Sentence Definition

Cervical myelopathy is spinal cord dysfunction, not routine neck pain, and should be suspected with gait disturbance, UMN signs, hand dysfunction, proprioceptive deficits, or bladder involvement.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter describes cervical myelopathy as the most common cervical cord lesion after middle age.
- Symptoms are often insidious and can include distal limb paresthesia, weakness, intrinsic hand wasting, gait disturbance, and bladder disturbance.
- Exam may show Hoffmann sign, brisk reflexes, Babinski sign, and proprioceptive / vibration deficits.
- Bladder disturbance suggests severe cord injury.
- Imaging stenosis must be interpreted with objective cord compromise and symptoms because asymptomatic stenosis exists.
- Nonoperative care is reserved only for mild, static cases without gait disturbance or pathologic reflexes.

#### Inferences

- Myelopathy screening belongs in neck and upper limb pain assessment even when the chief complaint is pain.
- Treating cervical myelopathy as a mechanical neck strain can delay decompression decisions.

### Concept: Whiplash and cervicogenic headache

#### Known Facts

- Most whiplash patients recover within 2-3 months; the chapter reports 82% symptom-free at two years.
- Acute whiplash management includes activity modification, cryotherapy, home exercise, PT, NSAID / acetaminophen, and education; opioids are discouraged.
- Chronic whiplash requires psychosocial and organic evaluation.
- Cervicogenic headache requires evidence of a cervical disorder and causation, such as temporal relation, reduced ROM / provocation, improvement with cervical disorder, or abolition by diagnostic block.

#### Inferences

- Imaging false positives make clinical causation criteria important in cervicogenic headache.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Always check Hoffmann and Babinski when cervical radicular symptoms are present.
- Soft collar use is described as short-term only; prolonged immobilization is not the main rehabilitation strategy.
- Traction is common but not strongly supported and is contraindicated in myelopathy, Lhermitte sign, rheumatoid arthritis, and atlantoaxial subluxation.
- Avoid treating cervical radiculopathy with deep heat ultrasound when inflammation may worsen nerve-root injury.
- Cervical transforaminal procedures are anatomically high-risk because of vertebral and radiculomedullary artery proximity.

## Research-Useful Points

- Future pages should separate cervical facet pain, cervicogenic headache, whiplash, cervical radiculopathy, and cervical myelopathy.

## Conflicts / Cautions

- The chapter's injection evidence includes older and mixed-quality studies; current procedural standards and safety recommendations should be checked before clinical use.
- Neck pain with upper limb symptoms is not automatically radiculopathy.
