# Source Summary: Complex regional pain syndrome in adults: Pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis

## Source Type

UpToDate topic review. Literature review current through 2026-04；topic last updated 2024-09-03。

## Reliability Level

Tier 6：expert topic review。可用於 adult CRPS 的定義、Budapest criteria、clinical manifestation、diagnostic testing limitations 與 differential diagnosis；不應替代 CRPS treatment guideline，也不應用於 pediatric CRPS。

## One-Sentence Summary

Adult CRPS is a regional, disproportionate, usually distal limb pain syndrome with sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/edema, motor/trophic features; diagnosis is clinical using Budapest criteria and exclusion of better alternatives, while bone scan, radiographs, autonomic testing, MRI/CT and sympathetic blocks are supportive or exclusionary rather than definitive.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: CRPS Budapest criteria and mimic exclusion

#### One-Sentence Definition

CRPS Budapest criteria 是 adult CRPS 的 clinical diagnostic framework：continuing disproportionate regional pain，加上 four symptom/sign domains 的最低門檻，並且沒有其他 diagnosis 更能解釋 presentation。

#### Known Facts

- CRPS usually affects a body region, often distal limbs.
- CRPS is characterized by pain, swelling, limited range of motion, vasomotor instability, skin changes, and patchy bone demineralization.
- It frequently begins after fracture, soft tissue injury, or surgery.
- Consensus definition describes continuing spontaneous and/or evoked regional pain that is disproportionate in time or degree to the usual course of trauma or lesion.
- CRPS pain is regional, not restricted to a specific nerve territory or dermatome, and usually has distal predominance of sensory, motor, sudomotor, vasomotor and/or trophic findings.
- Type I occurs without evidence of peripheral nerve injury and represents approximately 90 percent of clinical presentations.
- Type II refers to CRPS with peripheral nerve injury; the pathophysiologic basis and clinical utility of the type I/type II distinction are uncertain.
- Warm/cold and central/peripheral/mixed phenotypes have been proposed, but these are not the core diagnostic requirement.
- Pathogenesis is unknown; proposed mechanisms include peripheral inflammation/autoimmunity, neurogenic inflammation, catecholamine hypersensitivity, central sensitization, cortical reorganization, glial activation and genetics.
- Population-based incidence is reported around 5 to 26 per 100,000 per year, with female-to-male ratio 2:1 to 4:1.
- Most common inciting events include fractures, crush injuries, sprains and surgery; no precipitating factor is found in up to 10 percent.
- Earlier psychosocial/personality predisposition claims remain controversial and were not confirmed by later studies.
- Onset generally occurs within four to six weeks of the inciting event.
- Pain, sensory changes, motor impairments, autonomic symptoms and trophic changes are main clinical manifestations.
- Pain is usually the most prominent and debilitating symptom.
- Sensory findings may include hyperalgesia, allodynia or hypesthesia.
- Approximately two-thirds of patients have functional motor impairments related to pain; tremor, myoclonus, dystonic posture or impaired initiation can occur.
- Autonomic findings include skin temperature, color, sweating and edema asymmetry.
- The older three-stage model has largely been abandoned by most experts due to insufficient evidence for discrete stages.
- Diagnosis is based on history and physical examination; there is no gold-standard confirmatory test.
- Suspicion is raised when symptoms develop after limb trauma, are no longer fully explained by the initial trauma, and affect the distal limb or extend beyond the trauma region or a single nerve/root territory.
- Clinical Budapest criteria require continuing disproportionate pain, at least one symptom in three of four categories, at least one sign at evaluation in two of four categories, and no better diagnosis.
- Four Budapest domains are sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/edema and motor/trophic.
- Budapest criteria had sensitivity 82 percent and specificity 68 percent in a cohort cited by the source.
- Bone scintigraphy, radiography and autonomic testing can support diagnosis or exclude alternatives when clinical features are atypical, but CRPS remains a clinical diagnosis.
- Negative bone scan does not rule out CRPS.
- MRI can help exclude differential diagnoses but does not confirm CRPS.
- CT is not suggested as a diagnostic test.
- Positive response to sympathetic block is not diagnostic of CRPS; it may indicate sympathetically maintained pain.
- Differential diagnoses include infection, compartment syndrome, peripheral vascular disease, deep vein thrombosis, peripheral neuropathy, vascular thoracic outlet syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon, erythromelalgia, functional neurological symptom disorder and factitious disorder.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Fracture / soft tissue injury / surgery / nerve injury or unidentified trigger
-> peripheral inflammation + neurogenic inflammation + catecholamine hypersensitivity
-> peripheral and central sensitization + cortical sensorimotor reorganization
-> regional disproportionate pain + allodynia/hyperalgesia + vasomotor/sudomotor/motor/trophic changes
-> clinical CRPS pattern if Budapest criteria met and mimics excluded
```

#### Inferences

- CRPS diagnosis should be framed as a pattern-recognition plus exclusion process, not a test-driven diagnosis.
- The most important diagnostic safeguard is actively checking for infection, compartment syndrome, vascular disease, DVT and neuropathy before labeling CRPS.
- Type II should not be reduced to ordinary peripheral neuropathy; it is CRPS pattern plus a definable nerve injury.
- Psychological distress may coexist with CRPS, but this source does not support psychogenic causation as the default explanation.

#### Assumptions

- Adult patient, usually with limb pain after injury/surgery, being evaluated in a setting capable of physical examination and targeted exclusion of dangerous mimics.
- Clinician can examine both symptom reports and objective signs across the four Budapest domains.
- Treatment recommendations are intentionally deferred to a separate CRPS treatment source.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Pathogenesis remains unresolved and likely multifactorial.
- Type I/type II distinction has uncertain clinical utility.
- Warm/cold and central/peripheral/mixed phenotype categories are not yet routine treatment-selection rules in this source.
- Adjunctive tests can support or exclude but cannot confirm CRPS.
- Budapest criteria are more specific than older criteria but specificity remains imperfect.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Use Budapest criteria explicitly rather than vague "CRPS-like pain."
- Require both symptom history and objective signs at the time of evaluation.
- Always ask whether another diagnosis better explains the presentation.
- Negative bone scan, normal radiographs or nonconfirmatory MRI do not exclude CRPS if clinical criteria are otherwise met.
- MRI is mainly for alternative diagnosis exclusion.
- Sympathetic block response should be documented as possible sympathetically maintained pain, not proof of CRPS.
- Discrete stage labels are weakly supported and should not drive decision-making.

## Research-Useful Points

- Mechanistic work should separate peripheral inflammation, autoimmunity, catecholamine hypersensitivity, central sensitization, glial activation and cortical reorganization.
- Future phenotype research may clarify warm/cold or central/peripheral/mixed subtypes.
- Diagnostic testing research should be judged against clinical criteria plus outcome, not assumed to replace Budapest criteria.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- Conflicts with "CRPS can be confirmed by bone scan/MRI/CT."
- Conflicts with "sympathetic block response proves CRPS."
- Conflicts with "CRPS is simply a psychological/personality disorder."
- Conflicts with "CRPS always progresses through three stages."
- Conflicts with "regional limb pain after trauma equals CRPS without excluding infection, DVT, compartment syndrome or vascular disease."
