# Estimated Excess VCO2 and Performance Fatigability

## One-Sentence Definition

Estimated excess VCO2 and recovery VCO2 off-kinetics are CPET-derived exploratory measures that Wooten et al. tested as feasible indices of performance fatigability and as possible buffering-related moderators of recovery-fatigability relationships.

## Definition and Boundary

This page covers one concept across two separately ingested Wooten 2021 sources: whether estimated excess VCO2 and recovery VCO2 off-kinetics can be collected and related to performance fatigability measures.

It is not a validated clinical biomarker, not a lactate meter, not a direct acid-base chemistry measurement, and not a replacement for [[Gas_Exchange_Threshold]], [[V_Slope_Method]], or standard [[VO2_Kinetics]] interpretation.

## Why It Matters

- Performance fatigability is clinically relevant but difficult to quantify with a single stable measure.
- VO2 kinetics reflects aerobic response speed, but VCO2 may carry additional information about buffering and recovery.
- Wooten 2021 provides a small feasibility signal that recovery VCO2 off-kinetics, especially after controlled submaximal exercise, may track fatigability-related outcomes.
- The separate AET pilot adds a training-response signal: after 4 weeks of supervised cycling, estimated excess VCO2, endurance-bout time, and VCO2 recovery off-kinetics changed in the expected direction, but causality remains limited by the single-arm design.

## Preconditions or Conditions

- Peak treadmill CPET with AT identified by V-slope method.
- Submaximal constant work rate test at 80% of AT.
- Breath-by-breath gas exchange measurement.
- Recovery gas-exchange data for 10 minutes, excluding the first 20 seconds in kinetic fitting.
- Performance fatigability measured by VO2 on-kinetic ORI and PFSS from 10-min walk test.
- Interpretation restricted to apparently healthy adults in a small pilot sample.
- For the AET source: screened healthy adults, cycle ergometry, peak CPX before and after 4 weeks of supervised cycling, and recovery off-kinetic modeling after excluding the first 20 seconds.

## Mechanism

1. Exercise above the gas-exchange transition increases by-product and H+ handling demands.
2. Bicarbonate-related buffering can increase expired CO2 beyond metabolic CO2 from oxidative substrate use.
3. Wooten et al. estimated this excess VCO2 algebraically above AT.
4. After exercise, VCO2 off-kinetics may reflect the rate of return toward metabolic and acid-base homeostasis.
5. Slower or altered recovery kinetics could therefore relate to greater performance fatigability.
6. Aerobic exercise training could modify both above-AT tolerance and recovery kinetics through fitness and buffering-related adaptations.
7. In these sources, the relationship was observed statistically but not validated mechanistically with lactate, bicarbonate, or pH.

## Observable Patterns

- All 7 subjects completed peak CPET, CWRT, recovery periods, and 10-MWT without adverse events.
- Estimated excess VCO2 explained 61% of variability in VO2 on-kinetic ORI and 62% of variability in PFSS.
- CPET recovery VCO2-off ORI and Kt explained 70% and 73% of variability in VO2 on-kinetic ORI.
- Submaximal CWRT recovery VCO2-off ORI and Kt explained 93% and 96% of variability in performance fatigability in this sample.
- The authors considered the protocol feasible but recommended improved work-rate control for future fatigability testing.
- In the AET pilot, 20 participants completed 4 weeks of supervised cycling at 70 +/- 5% HRR for 45 min/session, 4 sessions/week.
- AET adherence was 99.7%.
- Peak VO2, peak VCO2, AT-VO2, Pk-Time, Pk-Watts, End1 time, and End2 time improved after AET.
- Estimated excess VCO2 increased from 2536 (1478) to 3144 (1680) mL, p = .001.
- VCO2 off-kinetic tau decreased from 81.0 (10.9) to 73.3 (10.6) seconds, p = .009.
- VCO2 off-kinetic MRT decreased from 102.8 (11.2) to 95.7 (10.5) seconds, p = .015.
- Recovery-fatigability correlations weakened after covarying estimated excess VCO2, which supports but does not prove a moderator role.

## Clinical / Research Implication

- Research implication: recovery VCO2 kinetics after controlled submaximal work is a plausible target for larger fatigability studies.
- Clinical implication: this concept can guide hypothesis generation and study design, but it should not be used as a standalone clinical decision marker.
- Method implication: the V-slope AT anchor and excess-VCO2 algorithm are assumptions that must be reported, not hidden as neutral preprocessing.
- Rehabilitation implication: recovery after exertion may be a meaningful outcome domain, especially when fatigue limits participation, but disease-specific safety and validation are still required.

## Fact

- The study enrolled 7 apparently healthy adults.
- The study used peak treadmill CPET and submaximal CWRT on separate visits.
- CWRT intensity was 80% of AT.
- AT was determined by V-slope method.
- Excess VCO2 was estimated, not directly measured.
- Lactate, bicarbonate, and pH were not measured.
- No adverse events occurred.
- The study's stated objective was feasibility for future larger studies.
- The AET source enrolled 21 subjects and had 20 completers.
- The AET source used 4 weeks of supervised cycling, 4 sessions/week, 45 minutes/session, at 70 +/- 5% HRR.
- The AET source reported 99.7% adherence.
- In the AET source, estimated excess VCO2 increased significantly after training.
- In the AET source, VCO2 off-kinetic tau and MRT improved significantly after training.
- In the AET source, plasma lactate and pH were not measured.
- The AET source was single-arm and nonrandomized.

## Inference

- VCO2 recovery kinetics may capture a recovery dimension that VO2 on-kinetics alone does not fully describe.
- Controlled submaximal testing may be a cleaner setting than a field walk test for studying physiological fatigability.
- The high R2 values are unstable until replicated in larger and more diverse samples.
- Correlation attenuation after covarying estimated excess VCO2 is compatible with moderation, but it does not prove causal mediation.
- The AET-associated increase in estimated excess VCO2 likely reflects greater above-AT exercise capacity and altered buffering demand rather than a simple isolated increase in acid production.

## Assumption

- Extending the pre-AT VCO2 relation estimates metabolic VCO2 above AT well enough to isolate excess VCO2.
- V-slope AT is an acceptable anchor for the algorithm.
- PFSS meaningfully reflects performance fatigability in this younger healthy sample.
- Pulmonary VCO2 recovery carries interpretable information about underlying acid-base and metabolic recovery.
- Time-to-exhaustion endurance bouts meaningfully reflect performance fatigability in the AET protocol.
- Training-related changes in estimated excess VCO2 can be interpreted as a buffering-related adaptation signal despite no direct pH or lactate measurement.

## Uncertainty

- Whether these associations persist in disease populations is unknown.
- Whether recovery VCO2 off-kinetics tracks lactate, bicarbonate, pH, or intracellular recovery was not tested.
- Whether 10-MWT PFSS is sensitive enough in young healthy adults is uncertain.
- Whether the same signal appears with cycle ergometry, other treadmill protocols, or different recovery durations is unknown.
- Whether the AET changes exceed repeated-testing or learning effects is uncertain because the AET source had no control group.
- Whether vigorous cycling at this dose is safe or effective in frail, cardiopulmonary, neuromuscular, or metabolic disease populations is not established by these sources.

## Limitations and Misreadings

- Do not label estimated excess VCO2 as directly measured buffering capacity.
- Do not treat this n=7 pilot as clinical validation.
- Do not treat the 20-completer single-arm AET pilot as randomized intervention evidence.
- Do not use the R2 values as expected effect sizes for routine CPET interpretation.
- Do not interpret AT language as proof of muscle dysoxia.
- Do not merge the two Wooten 2021 sources without preserving which facts came from which source.
