## One-Sentence Summary

Poststroke rehabilitation should analyze movement dysfunction by impairments, activity limitations, compensations, secondary complications, and task performance rather than attributing abnormal movement globally to spasticity or to the stroke diagnosis alone.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Poststroke movement dysfunction impairment model

#### One-Sentence Definition

Poststroke movement dysfunction is organized into primary neurologic impairments, secondary musculoskeletal / cardiopulmonary impairments, and composite movement problems that appear during functional tasks.

#### Known Facts

- Stroke / CVA is described as sudden specific neurologic deficit from vessel occlusion or rupture.
- Movement intervention should be directed by movement and activity limitations, not the diagnosis of stroke itself.
- Primary impairments include neurologic weakness, altered muscle activation, impaired initiation / cessation / sequencing / timing / force, sensory loss, and tone changes.
- Secondary impairments include cardiopulmonary deconditioning, alignment / mobility problems, muscle and soft tissue shortening, pain, and edema.
- Composite impairments include movement deficits, atypical movements, and undesirable compensations.
- Sensory processing, cognition, communication, affect, and arousal modify movement performance.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Stroke lesion
-> impaired neural drive, sensation, timing, force, cognition, and communication
-> primary movement impairment
-> inactivity, malalignment, pain, edema, shortening, deconditioning
-> secondary impairment
-> task-specific movement deficit, atypical movement, and compensation
```

#### Inferences

- Bedside movement observation should generate causal hypotheses and then test them with assisted movement and objective measures.
- Treating only tone may miss weakness, timing, sensory loss, postural control, pain, edema, and learned nonuse.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Recovery trajectories vary by lesion, severity, early return of movement, comorbidities, rehabilitation access, and complications.

### Concept: Spasticity vs hypertonicity after stroke

#### One-Sentence Definition

Spasticity is velocity-dependent reflex overactivity during passive stretch, while hypertonicity is broader resistance to passive movement and should not be treated as the same construct.

#### Known Facts

- The source states the older belief that spasticity causes atypical movement patterns has been refuted.
- Spasticity is a velocity-dependent reflex in a passive state.
- Hypertonicity is often used as a synonym for spasticity, but the source states this is incorrect.
- Poststroke movement dysfunction can occur from severe neurologic weakness even without hypertonicity.
- Centrally acting antispasticity medications can cause CNS depression, drowsiness, or anxiety that may interfere with movement therapy.
- BoNT-A produces temporary selective weakness and is most useful when followed by intensive muscle reeducation and splinting in the source.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
UMN lesion
-> abnormal reflex excitability may cause velocity-dependent spasticity
-> passive resistance may also reflect hypertonicity, contracture, stiffness, pain, or poor alignment
-> functional movement problem requires separating reflex from nonreflex causes
```

#### Inferences

- Tone management should be tied to a functional goal, not to reducing Modified Ashworth score alone.
- Weakness and impaired selective activation should be actively assessed before escalating tone-directed treatment.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This source gives principles but does not replace current procedural dosing guidance, medication monitoring, or individualized risk assessment.

### Concept: Movement reeducation and compensation

#### One-Sentence Definition

Poststroke motor reeducation aims to restore task-specific movement through active practice, fading assistance, sensory-motor feedback, and careful use of compensation only when needed for independence or safety.

#### Known Facts

- Undesirable compensations include excessive reliance on the uninvolved limbs and asymmetric trunk patterns.
- Such compensations can be unsafe, contribute to secondary impairments, reinforce learned nonuse, and hinder future recovery.
- Limiting compensatory trunk movement may increase arm-reaching performance in the source.
- Motor reeducation requires patient-initiated active movement, assistance that fades over time, and progression toward independent practice.
- No single physical rehabilitation intervention is described as superior; physical rehabilitation is effective as a whole.
- The source supports a combination approach: remediate trunk-extremity patterns, minimize secondary impairments, teach compensation when needed, and practice functional movement in tasks.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Active attempted movement
-> therapist assistance corrects alignment, reduces degrees of freedom, assists weak muscle, stabilizes joints
-> repetition with feedback and task relevance
-> improved activation, timing, postural control, and functional task performance
```

#### Inferences

- Compensation is not automatically wrong; the problem is compensation that replaces recovery-oriented activation when recovery potential and safety allow.
- Safety, independence, and impaired-side activation must be balanced explicitly.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Optimal dose, timing, and combination of specific poststroke interventions vary by stage, severity, impairment profile, and setting.

## Clinical Implications

- Evaluate poststroke movement in task context: initiation, execution / transition, completion / termination, posture, extremity skill, pain, edema, and caregiver / environmental demands.
- Do not use “no pain, no gain” in neurorehabilitation; pain suggests alignment or movement mechanics need correction in the source.
- Shoulder pain management should address scapular mobility, humeral alignment / rotation, edema, desensitization, and pain-free graded movement.
- Outcome measures cited for stroke rehabilitation include 6MWT, 10-meter walk, BBS, Functional Gait Assessment, ABC Scale, 5xSTS, Orpington Prognostic Scale, PASS, STREAM, Fugl-Meyer, FIM, and Stroke Impact Scale.
- Cardiovascular training after stroke is described as safe and beneficial across phases in a 2020 Cochrane review cited by the source, especially when regular, progressive, and moderate to high intensity.

## Fact

- The source states ischemic stroke accounts for the majority of strokes and lists hemorrhagic stroke subtypes separately.
- Hypertension is described as the foremost modifiable stroke risk factor.
- The source states that 75-80% of people after stroke have insufficient force production.
- Postural control includes anticipatory and compensatory adjustments; anticipatory sequence may be preserved but timing impaired after stroke.
- Hemiplegic walking consumes more oxygen than typical walking in the source.

## Inference

- A stroke rehabilitation note that only states “hemiparesis with spasticity” is under-specified for treatment planning.
- Assisted movement is a diagnostic maneuver as well as a treatment technique because it tests alignment, weakness, selective activation, and compensatory strategy.

## Assumption

- The movement analysis is performed after medical stability and acute stroke safety restrictions are accounted for.

## Uncertainty

- Stroke recovery prediction is probabilistic; early movement return is useful but not sufficient to determine individual potential.
- This source does not provide current acute treatment eligibility rules or contemporary stroke prevention medication recommendations.
