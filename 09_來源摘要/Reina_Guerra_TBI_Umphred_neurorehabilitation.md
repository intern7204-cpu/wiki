## One-Sentence Summary

Moderate to severe TBI rehabilitation should be organized as an ICF-based, phase-specific continuum that links injury severity, consciousness recovery, task analysis, medical precautions, interprofessional care, task-oriented training, and long-term participation support.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: TBI ICF照護連續體與Task Analysis

#### One-Sentence Definition

TBI rehabilitation planning should move from health condition and prognosis to body functions, activities, participation, environmental / personal factors, and task analysis, then select phase-specific interventions across acute, inpatient, outpatient, and lifelong care.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter focuses on rehabilitation for persons with moderate to severe TBI; concussion / mTBI is handled elsewhere.
- TBI is distinguished from other acquired brain injuries such as stroke and anoxic brain injury because pathophysiology and recovery course differ.
- TBI is defined around a sudden event caused by an external force that disrupts normal brain function.
- Mechanisms include direct external force, acceleration-deceleration, blast injury, and penetrating injury.
- Primary injury refers to the initial mechanical insult.
- Secondary injury refers to biochemical, cellular, and physiologic events after primary injury.
- Secondary injury contributors include increased ICP, cerebral hypoxia / ischemia, intracranial hemorrhage, electrolyte / acid-base imbalance, infection, seizure, and neuroinflammation.
- The chapter lists pediatric anatomical / physiologic differences that complicate medical management and recovery.
- The developing brain's neuroplasticity does not mean pediatric TBI has benign prognosis; injury in infants and toddlers can limit later skill development.
- The chapter states that approximately 62% of children have lingering cognitive and behavioral difficulties after childhood severe TBI, negatively affecting education and social participation.
- The chapter states that timely and aggressive inpatient rehabilitation has been shown to be a significant factor improving functional outcomes in children with moderate to severe TBI.
- Prognostic indicators include age, clinical severity, pupil reactivity, CT abnormalities, secondary insults, laboratory values, duration of coma, posttraumatic amnesia, and time to follow commands.
- No single instrument can accurately predict TBI outcome.
- GCS is useful but should be repeated and interpreted with other indicators.
- In sedated or paralyzed patients, pupillary reflex may be a better prognostic indicator than GCS.
- CRASH and IMPACT models use early variables to predict outcomes, but criticisms include lack of treatment-response and secondary-insult considerations.
- Duration of posttraumatic amnesia is a functional prognostic indicator.
- The chapter states that if PTA lasts less than 4 weeks, severe activity limitation is unlikely; if PTA lasts longer than 8 weeks, good recovery is unlikely when measured by GOS.
- Time to Follow Commands is an important predictor of injury severity, consciousness recovery, and functional outcome.
- Long-term outcomes after moderate to severe TBI commonly include impairments, activity limitations, and participation restrictions.
- The chapter cites TBI Model Systems data: around 30% of persons with moderate to severe TBI worsen within 5 years, around 26% stay home, around 22% die, and around 57% of living persons have moderate to severe disability.
- Five-year unemployment after TBI is reported at 55% in the cited TBI Model Systems data.
- The chapter explicitly uses the WHO ICF as the framework for examination, evaluation, intervention, and outcomes across care environments.
- TBI outcomes depend not only on location and severity but also prior function, support systems, timing, and quality of healthcare interventions.
- Examination / evaluation history should include mechanism / nature of injury, initial GCS, injury date, age, medical interventions, duration of unconsciousness, stage of consciousness, and overall recovery progress.
- Education, family support, living circumstances, complications, comorbid disease, and coexisting injuries inform prognosis and discharge planning.
- Phase-specific examination shifts from body functions in coma / VS / RLA I-II, to inconsistent command following in RLA III / MCS, to agitation and confusion management in RLA IV-VI, then toward activity and participation as consciousness improves.
- Recommended outcome measures include tools such as CRS-R, ABS, Moss Attention Rating Scale, Rancho Levels of Cognitive Functioning, FIM / FAM, Barthel Index, 6-minute walk test, 10 m walk test, Berg Balance Scale, Community Balance and Mobility Scale, HiMAT, DRS, PHQ, and participation / quality-of-life measures.
- For activity limitations, task analysis links observed performance problems to underlying body-function hypotheses.
- The chapter gives two caveats: improving abnormal components may not improve activity limitations, and treating impairments alone does not necessarily teach a skill.
- Whole-task practice is necessary for skill learning.
- Early care priorities include preservation of life and neural tissue, prevention of secondary complications, early mobility, and improving arousal / awareness in patients with disorders of consciousness.
- Across settings, physical therapists should prevent pneumonia, pressure sores, adaptive shortening, disuse atrophy, HO, joint contractures, and DVT.
- Acute therapists must monitor devices, precautions, lab values, and vital signs to maintain safety.
- Step-down / acute neuro care shifts toward mobility and meaningful interactions with the environment.
- Inpatient or subacute rehabilitation prioritizes safety and independence with functional mobility, ADLs, and appropriate interaction.
- Postrehab goals focus on community reintegration, return to school / work / family responsibilities, independent function, and lifelong supports.
- Interprofessional rehabilitation is emphasized because TBI creates physical, cognitive, behavioral, psychological, communication, visual, and social participation problems.
- Environmental stimulation can help manage delirium, confusion, agitation, PSH triggers, sleep-wake cycle, and daytime arousal.
- Cognitive impairment requires consistency, repetition, team communication, and incorporation of communication, memory, orientation, and behavioral strategies across disciplines.
- For DoC, management includes preventing secondary complications, managing spasticity, upright positioning, sensory stimulation, pain observation, and seizure vigilance.
- Sensory stimulation is routinely used for DoC, but systematic reviews have not found strong evidence because of poor study quality, variable designs, and inconsistent outcome measures.
- For motor recovery, considerable evidence supports that persons with TBI make functional gains in rehabilitation, but research on specific intervention types is less robust than in stroke.
- Treadmill training after TBI is not clearly superior to overground training.
- VR evidence in TBI is emerging but limited; benefits may partly reflect motivation and repetition, and conventional therapy groups often achieve similar outcomes.
- Visual-vestibular examination should be included after TBI because peripheral, central, or mixed vestibular pathology can occur and may be underrecognized.
- Aerobic exercise has potential benefits for neuroplasticity, physical function, cognition, mood, sleep, and secondary health prevention, but timing and intensity after acute TBI require caution.
- Dual-task deficits can persist even when gait speed appears normal; dual-task training should be specific to life-role and environmental demands.
- NBS / TMS / tDCS remain emerging for TBI, with seizure risk and limited evidence requiring caution.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Moderate/severe TBI
-> primary injury + secondary injury cascade
-> altered consciousness, cognition, behavior, motor control, sensation, vestibular function, pain, endurance, communication, and psychosocial function
-> ICF-based examination maps body functions, activities, participation, environmental factors, and personal factors
-> phase-specific assessment matches current recovery state and care environment
-> task analysis identifies which impairments actually limit target activities
-> intervention prioritizes safety, secondary-complication prevention, arousal, mobility, task-oriented practice, dual-task / participation demands, and long-term supports
```

#### Inferences

- A TBI rehab plan should not start by listing exercises; it should start with phase of recovery, medical stability, task priorities, prognosis variables, and participation goals.
- Activity-level goals require task analysis because the most visible impairment may not be the limiting impairment.
- High-functioning gait after TBI should be stress-tested with environmental and cognitive demands; normal straight-line gait speed is insufficient.
- Pediatric TBI needs developmental surveillance because deficits can emerge when new school, executive, social, or motor demands appear later.

#### Assumptions

- The care team has access to phase-appropriate assessment tools and can repeat them over time.
- Interventions are adjusted to medical precautions, device limits, ICP / vital sign parameters, pain, seizure risk, cognition, behavior, and staffing.
- The goal is not only discharge mobility but long-term participation and health management.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This is a textbook chapter and should not replace updated Brain Trauma Foundation, AAN / ACRM, INCOG, pediatric TBI, or local acute-care protocols.
- Several interventions, including VR, robotic gait, NBS, sensory stimulation, and optimal aerobic exercise timing / intensity, have limited or heterogeneous evidence.
- Prognostic models can discriminate risk at a population level but do not fully account for treatment response, secondary insults, family support, access to rehabilitation, or individual goals.
- Long-term chronic brain injury concepts and periodic therapy models need continued evidence development.

## Clinically Useful Points

- In moderate to severe TBI, document mechanism, GCS trajectory, pupil findings, CT abnormalities, secondary insults, duration of unconsciousness, PTA, Time to Follow Commands, age, comorbidities, procedures, complications, and recovery trajectory.
- Use the current phase of recovery to choose examination targets: body functions in RLA I-II, reproducible response / MCS features around RLA III, agitation / confusion in RLA IV-VI, activity and participation when consciousness improves.
- Use task analysis before treating an activity limitation; ask which body-function impairment is actually constraining the activity.
- Include participation outcomes early: home role, school / work, family responsibility, driving, community mobility, leisure, and social interaction.
- Do not assume that treating tone, strength, balance, or cognition in isolation automatically produces task skill.
- For chronic TBI, revisit exercise, sleep, mood, cognition, pain, vestibular symptoms, endurance, community integration, and caregiver needs periodically.

## Research-Useful Points

- TBI rehab research should separate phase of recovery, injury mechanism, activity target, task specificity, dose, timing, intensity, environment, and participation outcomes.
- Trials should distinguish impairment change from activity learning and real-world participation transfer.
- Pediatric TBI studies should follow developmental demands over time rather than only early physical recovery.
- Emerging technologies should be compared against dose-matched conventional therapy and measured with functional / participation outcomes.
