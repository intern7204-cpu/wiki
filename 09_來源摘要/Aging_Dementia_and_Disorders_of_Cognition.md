## One-Sentence Summary

This chapter frames dementia care as stage-aware, person-centered rehabilitation: cognitive impairment must be distinguished from normal aging, delirium, and depression, while management must target function, environment, caregiver support, safety, exercise, meaningful activity, and quality of life.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: 正常老化與 pathological cognitive decline

#### One-Sentence Definition

Normal aging can involve measurable brain and executive-function changes without necessarily causing activity limitation, while pathological cognitive decline implies clinically meaningful deterioration beyond expected aging.

#### Known Facts

- Healthy aging is associated with cortical volume / thickness changes and network reorganization.
- The source states that these changes do not necessarily produce overt cognitive impairment with activity limitation or participation restriction.
- Memory, language, and visuospatial function may remain relatively stable in healthy older adults, while executive function is more vulnerable.
- Older adults may struggle more with fast-paced, unfamiliar, complex tasks.
- Neuroplastic changes can continue into old age, and enriched environments may improve brain volume, connectivity, and cognition.
- Cognitive and neural reserve are proposed explanations for interindividual variability.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Aging-related structural / network change
-> variable effect on executive function and processing demands
-> reserve, activity, environment, and pacing modify performance
-> functional cognition may remain adequate despite aging
```

#### Inferences

- Cognitive complaints in older adults should not be dismissed as normal aging before functional impact and reversible causes are assessed.
- Rehabilitation should modify task pace, complexity, context, and environmental support before assuming fixed incapacity.

#### Assumptions

- The person is not acutely delirious, severely depressed, intoxicated, or affected by medication / metabolic causes when aging-related cognition is being interpreted.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This source supports the concept of reserve but does not provide a deterministic rule for predicting individual decline.

### Concept: Delirium, depression, and dementia differentiation

#### One-Sentence Definition

Delirium is acute and fluctuating, depression may mimic cognitive decline, and dementia is a progressive neurocognitive disorder with increasing functional dependence.

#### Known Facts

- Neurocognitive disorders in the source include delirium, mild cognitive impairment / minor neurocognitive disorder, and dementia / major neurocognitive disorder.
- Major and minor neurocognitive disorders are differentiated by severity and interference with everyday independence.
- Delirium involves acute disturbance in attention, environmental awareness, cognition, sleep-wake regulation, and emotion, with fluctuating severity.
- Delirium requires early medical assessment and treatment.
- Depression in older adults may present with pain, weakness, headache, agitation, fatigue, appetite / weight change, constipation, insomnia or hypersomnia, and irritability.
- Geriatric Depression Scale cutoffs in the source: more than 11 on the 30-item form or more than 5 on the 15-item form suggests possible depression.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Acute medical / medication / sensory / pain / retention trigger
-> attention and arousal disturbance
-> fluctuating cognition and behavior
-> delirium until proven otherwise
```

#### Inferences

- In rehabilitation, sudden cognitive or participation decline should be treated as a medical signal, not simply poor motivation or irreversible dementia.
- Depression screening is necessary when dementia-like symptoms are disproportionate to observed functional history.

#### Assumptions

- Screening tools are being used as screens, not diagnostic substitutes for medical and neuropsychological evaluation.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- The source lists screening options but does not define one universal tool for all settings, education levels, languages, and dementia severities.

### Concept: Dementia rehabilitation and caregiver support

#### One-Sentence Definition

Dementia rehabilitation aims to preserve meaningful activity, safety, mobility, ADL participation, quality of life, and caregiver capacity through stage-specific, person-centered, nonpharmacologic care.

#### Known Facts

- Dementia is progressive and affects cognition, ADLs, emotions, relationships, and care needs.
- Early dementia is often overlooked because independence may be preserved and symptoms are gradual.
- Middle stage is often the longest; IADLs and later basic ADLs decline, and behavioral issues become more evident.
- Late stage involves severe physical and cognitive disability with total dependence.
- GDS and FAST are staging tools; FAST stage progression emphasizes clothing choice, dressing, bathing, toileting, incontinence, speech, ambulation, sitting, smiling, and head control.
- Patient-centered dementia care includes knowing the person, recognizing the person’s reality, supporting meaningful engagement, building caring relationships, creating supportive community, and evaluating care practices.
- Nonpharmacologic treatment includes exercise / motor rehabilitation, cognitive intervention, OT, psychological therapy, stimulation therapy, complementary approaches, and technology.
- OT interventions include activity simplification, environmental modification, adaptive aids, problem-solving, skill training, and caregiver education.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Progressive cognitive decline
-> impaired judgment, planning, memory, communication, and mobility
-> IADL / ADL dependence and caregiver burden
-> stage-specific task simplification, environmental support, exercise, and caregiver training
-> preserved safety, participation, and quality of life where possible
```

#### Inferences

- Dementia rehabilitation should measure function and participation, not only cognitive test score.
- Caregiver education is part of the intervention, not an optional add-on.

#### Assumptions

- Interventions are tailored to dementia stage, communication ability, safety risk, caregiver capacity, and meaningful life history.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- The source notes no consensus on the best nonpharmacologic combination because interventions, dementia stages, patients, and caregivers are heterogeneous.

## Clinical Implications

- Baseline cognitive and functional status should be documented before rehabilitation goals are set.
- A positive cognitive screen should lead to more formal assessment rather than immediate dementia labeling.
- Delirium, depression, medication effects, pain, constipation, urinary retention, infection, metabolic disorder, and sensory overload / deprivation are high-yield reversible contributors.
- Dementia care should use [[ICF_功能框架]]: body structure / function, activity, participation, environment, and caregiver context.
- Physical performance measures in mild to moderate dementia can include gait speed, 6MWT / 2MWT, TUG, 5xSTS, SPPB, BBS, Functional Reach, grip strength, and ADL measures; severe dementia has fewer optimal measurement options.

## Fact

- Dementia prevalence rises with age.
- Alzheimer disease is the most common dementia cause in the source.
- The source lists AD8, Mini-Cog, MoCA, GPCOG, MIS, SLUMS, verbal fluency, Clock Drawing, and other tools as cognitive screens.
- The Alzheimer’s Association workgroup cited in the source identifies MIS, GPCOG, and Mini-Cog as brief primary-care tools with less than 5-minute administration and no fee / permission requirement.
- The AAN MCI guidance cited in the source states that patient or close-contact cognitive concern should be assessed and not assumed to be normal aging.

## Inference

- Cognitive screening should be chosen based on setting, time, language / education effects, and the clinical question.
- A rehabilitation plan for dementia is incomplete if it ignores caregiver training, task simplification, environmental modification, and fall / mobility risk.

## Assumption

- The source’s prevention and medication sections reflect the publication context of the textbook chapter and may not represent the latest guideline status.

## Uncertainty

- The causal effect of single-domain exercise or cognitive intervention on dementia prevention remains less certain than observational associations.
- Antiamyloid therapy status, eligibility, safety monitoring, and clinical effect require current external verification.
