# Source Summary: Muir et al. 2023 — Preschool SR/EF Interventions

## Source Type

Systematic literature review.

- Source title: *Interventions and Approaches Targeting Early Self-Regulation or Executive Functioning in Preschools: A Systematic Review*
- Authors: Rosalyn A. Muir, Steven J. Howard, Lisa Kervin.
- Publication year: 2023.
- Source file: `C:\原始資料\s10648-023-09740-6\s10648-023-09740-6.md`

## Reliability Level

- Source tier: Tier 1.
- Reason: systematic review using PRISMA methodology, registered with PROSPERO, with structured extraction of intervention type, sample, dose, outcome, effect size, and risk of bias.
- Confidence: medium-high for mapping intervention design features; medium for approach-specific claims because studies were heterogeneous and many had small samples.

## One-Sentence Summary

Preschool SR/EF interventions show potential, but the best-supported design logic is not a single programme type; it is matching the target outcome to cognitively challenging, engaging, developmentally appropriate, feedback-rich, and feasible classroom implementation.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Preschool SR/EF Intervention Design Characteristics

#### One-Sentence Definition

Preschool SR/EF intervention design should be judged by the fit between target outcome, child group, cognitive challenge, movement / engagement, feedback, interventionist expertise, dose, fidelity, and sustainability rather than by the broad label of the programme.

#### Known Facts

- The review examined interventions designed to enhance self-regulation and/or executive function in preschool settings.
- The review followed PRISMA-P guidelines and was registered with PROSPERO.
- The literature search was performed on 2020-09-28 and re-searched in January 2021.
- Included studies were peer-reviewed English-language intervention studies published from 2000 to 2020.
- Included children were typically developing preschool children aged 3-6 years in preschool settings.
- Studies exclusively focused on children with documented disabilities such as Attention Deficit Disorder were excluded.
- Individual studies required a control group, pre/post measures, and validated SR/EF outcome measures.
- The search initially generated 9115 results; 3288 duplicates were identified; 641 full texts were obtained; 85 studies were included.
- The 85 studies sampled 12,595 children.
- 72 of 85 studies (84.7%) were published between 2015 and 2020.
- Sample sizes ranged from 20 to 877, with mean sample size 148; 48 studies had fewer than 100 children.
- External facilitators led 52 studies (61.17%).
- 23 countries were represented; most studies were from North America or Europe.
- 59 studies (69.4%) exclusively targeted one or more EF abilities; 17 exclusively targeted SR; 9 targeted both SR and EF.
- Median intervention dose was 9 hours; dose ranged from a single 15-minute period to year-long curricula.
- Measurement varied: 47 studies used only task-based measures, 10 used only behaviour-rating questionnaires, and 20 used both.
- Head, Toes, Knees, Shoulders was the most frequently used task-based measure.
- Statistically significant results for at least one outcome were reported in 60 studies (70.6%).
- Across 208 evaluated effects, 102 (49.0%) were statistically significant immediately after intervention.
- Only four studies included follow-up assessments several months after the intervention.
- The review classified interventions into 4 higher-order categories and 12 approaches: play, social-emotional, curricula / pedagogy, and non-routine activities.
- Mediated structured play included 16 studies; 12 reported statistically significant acute effects, and 26 of 41 evaluated effects were significant.
- Mindfulness included 12 studies; 10 reported significant outcomes, and 17 of 26 evaluated effects were significant.
- SEL programmes included 8 studies; 6 reported at least one significant outcome, and 11 of 23 evaluated effects were significant.
- Digital task training included 9 studies; 5 reported at least one significant EF outcome, and 7 of 21 evaluated effects were significant.
- Physical activity included 12 studies; 10 reported at least one significant outcome, and 14 of 20 evaluated effects were significant.
- Cognitive challenge was present in all interventions identified as most efficacious.
- Movement appeared in 53 studies (62.35%); 77.4% of these interventions achieved one or more significant effects.
- 23 movement-including interventions were classified as highly efficacious, representing 63.88% of all high-efficacy interventions.
- The review suggests small groups may balance social interaction / co-regulation with more individualized challenge than whole-class or individual digital approaches.
- Teacher-led higher-efficacy studies tended to include more substantial professional development, spaced training, or implementation support.
- No clear perfect dosage emerged across approaches.
- Practice with feedback may support reflection, metacognition, and adjustment of automatic responses.
- The review did not conduct meta-analysis because interventions and approaches were highly heterogeneous and the aim was to identify characteristics of effective interventions.
- Fidelity was often unreported and not evaluated as a common criterion across studies.
- The authors concluded that there is no clear consensus or evidence for the particular efficacy of one approach over others.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Preschool child practises SR/EF demands in an engaging context
-> activity includes incremental cognitive challenge, movement / social engagement, and feedback
-> child must monitor, inhibit, remember rules, shift, plan, and adjust responses
-> repeated practice supports acute SR/EF task or behaviour gains
-> long-term transfer depends on fidelity, context, dose, teacher capacity, child baseline, and sustained practice
```

#### Inferences

- The design feature matters more than the programme label: a play, mindfulness, SEL, physical activity, or curriculum approach can work only if it creates the right SR/EF learning conditions.
- Physical activity effects likely depend on cognitively engaging movement, not just more movement volume.
- Digital training may be appropriate for working memory practice, but it should not be assumed to improve global classroom self-regulation.
- For preschool classroom implementation, teacher training and coaching are not accessory details; they are part of the active delivery mechanism.

#### Assumptions

- Measures capture clinically or educationally meaningful SR/EF change rather than only task familiarity.
- Teachers or external facilitators can implement the intervention with adequate fidelity.
- The classroom has enough staffing, time, space, and leadership support to sustain practice.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Follow-up data were rare, so persistence and fadeout remain unclear.
- Study heterogeneity prevents simple pooled comparison across intervention approaches.
- Fidelity was often not reported in a common format.
- Demographic reporting was inconsistent; sample diversity effects could not be fully interpreted.
- Many approach-specific conclusions rely on limited study numbers and sometimes small samples.
- The review included typically developing preschool populations, so it should not be directly generalized to ADHD, ASD, intellectual disability, language disorder, trauma-exposed, or complex clinical populations.

## Clinically Useful Points

- When a preschool child has regulation or classroom-readiness concerns, ask what SR/EF component is targeted: working memory, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, planning, global EF, behavioural SR, or classroom behaviour.
- A useful SR/EF activity should include progressively harder rules or demands, enough repetition, feedback, and real engagement.
- Small-group formats may be especially plausible because they combine co-regulation and social practice with more individualized challenge.
- Do not equate a commercial app or digital working-memory task with broad school-readiness intervention.
- If teachers deliver the intervention, document training, coaching, fidelity, and how practice is embedded in daily routines.

## Research-Useful Points

- Future studies should report fidelity, implementation burden, dose, teacher training, coaching, child baseline moderators, longer follow-up, and transfer outcomes.
- Studies should separate trained task gains from broader classroom, academic, social-emotional, and participation outcomes.
- Comparative research should test active components rather than comparing broad labels such as "play" versus "mindfulness" versus "physical activity".
