## One-Sentence Summary

Neurological disorders affect sexuality through direct lesion effects, secondary body-function and medication effects, and tertiary psychosocial / participation effects; rehabilitation assessment must deliberately separate these layers instead of treating sexual dysfunction as only erectile dysfunction or only a psychosocial issue.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Neurogenic Sexual Dysfunction 三層影響框架

#### One-Sentence Definition

Neurogenic sexual dysfunction should be formulated as the interaction of primary neural pathway disruption, secondary impairment / comorbidity / medication effects, and tertiary psychosocial / relationship / participation barriers.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter explicitly classifies neurological effects on sexual function into primary, secondary, and tertiary impacts.
- Primary impacts are direct effects of the neurological lesion on sexual function.
- Secondary impacts are effects on other body functions involved in sexuality and side effects of medications.
- Tertiary impacts are psychosocial aspects that reduce access to social contacts and interfere with sexual relationships.
- Male reflexogenic erection depends on genital stimulation mediated through S2-S4.
- Male psychogenic erection can run through sacral pathways or thoracolumbar T11-L2 pathways.
- Ejaculation includes emission and expulsion.
- Emission is controlled by thoracolumbar innervation; expulsion is controlled by sacral spinal segments and perineal nerves.
- A spinal generator of ejaculation is described at L3-L4.
- Female sexual response involves genital reflex pathways and broader motivational, emotional, experiential, and developmental factors.
- SCI is the most studied neurological condition for sexual function in this chapter.
- In SCI, sexual outcomes vary by lesion level and completeness.
- Higher SCI lesions tend to preserve reflexogenic erections more than psychogenic erections; lower sacral lesions tend to impair reflexogenic erection.
- SCI-related ejaculation potential depends on preservation of thoracolumbar, sacral, and lumbar spinal connections.
- Orgasm can occur after SCI even without ejaculation.
- Autonomic dysreflexia should be considered in SCI when ejaculation or orgasm is associated with severe hypertension or headache.
- MS sexual dysfunction commonly involves desire, genital sensation, arousal / lubrication, erectile / ejaculatory / orgasmic dysfunction, fatigue, spasticity, bladder / bowel symptoms, pain, medication effects, mood, cognition, and relationship quality.
- Stroke sexual dysfunction may involve hypoactive desire, reduced intercourse frequency, erectile / ejaculatory dysfunction, decreased lubrication, diminished orgasm, motor limitation, sensory changes, pain, fatigue, fear of another stroke, depression, and partner dynamics.
- TBI may produce hyposexuality, erectile dysfunction, orgasmic problems, reduced satisfaction, or inappropriate / impulsive sexual behavior.
- Pituitary deficits after TBI are reported in the chapter as common enough to consider hormonal contribution.
- PD sexual problems can include decreased or increased libido, arousal disorder, erectile dysfunction, orgasm dysfunction, hypersexuality, and compulsive sexual behaviors.
- Dopaminergic therapy in PD can improve motor symptoms but may also contribute to hypersexuality or impulse-control problems in susceptible patients.
- Treatments discussed include PDE5 inhibitors, intracavernous injections, intraurethral prostaglandin / alprostadil, penile rings / vacuum devices, penile prostheses, penile vibrostimulation, midodrine-assisted ejaculation protocols in SCI, electroejaculation for fertility, lubricants, clitoral vacuum devices, vibrostimulation, positioning strategies, adaptive equipment, pain / fatigue / spasticity planning, medication review, psychosocial intervention, education, peer support, CBT-style strategies, and relationship counseling.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Neurological disorder
-> primary disruption of sacral, thoracolumbar, lumbar, autonomic, sensory, motor, cortical, limbic, or dopaminergic sexual pathways
-> impaired desire, arousal, erection, lubrication, ejaculation, orgasm, genital sensation, or sexual behavior regulation
-> secondary interference from bladder / bowel dysfunction, spasticity, pain, fatigue, mobility limits, autonomic dysreflexia, skin risk, medical comorbidity, and medications
-> tertiary effects on body image, self-esteem, intimacy, partner communication, social access, mood, and participation
-> sexual concern becomes a rehabilitation problem when it affects distress, safety, relationship function, quality of life, or participation
```

#### Inferences

- A sexual-function complaint after SCI, MS, stroke, TBI, or PD should be formulated across all three layers before choosing a treatment.
- Sexual medicine referral and pharmacologic treatment can be necessary, but they are incomplete if secondary and tertiary barriers are not addressed.
- In neurorehabilitation, asking about sexuality is part of function and participation assessment, not an optional lifestyle detail.
- Female sexual concerns require direct assessment of desire, arousal, lubrication, pain, orgasm, satisfaction, relationship context, and neurologic sensory / autonomic factors rather than extrapolation from male erectile dysfunction.

#### Assumptions

- The patient experiences the sexual change as distressing, unsafe, relationship-limiting, or participation-limiting.
- The clinical team can discuss sexuality in a private, nonjudgmental, medically precise way.
- Red flags such as autonomic dysreflexia, cardiovascular instability, severe genital / pelvic pain, active pressure injury, unsafe medication interaction, abuse, coercion, or uncontrolled psychiatric symptoms are screened before routine sexual rehabilitation advice.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This chapter is not a formal guideline and does not grade every intervention with systematic certainty.
- Many condition-specific prevalence and treatment statements come from heterogeneous studies.
- Stroke lesion-location associations with sexual dysfunction are presented cautiously because evidence is mixed.
- Novel erectile dysfunction treatments such as PRP, stem-cell therapy, and low-intensity shockwave therapy are not established for neurological populations in this chapter.
- Female neurogenic sexual dysfunction treatments have less consistent evidence than male erectile dysfunction treatments.
- The chapter does not replace individualized urology, gynecology, sexual medicine, psychology, psychiatry, endocrinology, fertility, or cardiology evaluation when indicated.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Start with a three-layer formulation: primary neural pathway, secondary impairment / medication / comorbidity, tertiary psychosocial / relationship / participation.
- In SCI, document lesion level, completeness, autonomic dysreflexia risk, bladder / bowel program, pressure injury risk, spasticity, pain, and preserved reflexogenic / psychogenic responses.
- In MS, ask about fatigue, spasticity, bladder / bowel symptoms, pain, cognition, depression / anxiety, relationship quality, and medication effects.
- In stroke, assess fear of recurrence, mood, hemiparesis, neglect, sensory change, pain, fatigue, cardiometabolic comorbidity, antihypertensive effects, and partner concerns.
- In TBI, screen for both reduced sexual function and inappropriate sexual behavior; consider impulse control, cognition, communication, mood, social participation, and possible hormonal contribution.
- In PD, ask separately about decreased sexual function and dopaminergic treatment-related hypersexuality / impulse-control symptoms.
- Treat secondary barriers with rehab-specific tools: positioning, equipment, wheelchair / bed strategies, skin inspection, spasticity management, bowel / bladder planning, fatigue pacing, pain timing, and medication review.
- Treat tertiary barriers with education, communication support, relationship work, peer resources, social participation, CBT-style strategies, and referral when needed.

## Research-Useful Points

- Future evidence should separate primary neurologic impairment from secondary and tertiary contributors rather than reporting undifferentiated sexual dysfunction prevalence.
- Female neurogenic sexual dysfunction needs better condition-specific intervention studies.
- Stroke lesion-location claims, TBI endocrine contribution, and PD treatment-related hypersexuality need careful measurement because mechanisms are heterogeneous.
- Rehabilitation outcome research should include distress, satisfaction, relationship function, safety, participation, and quality of life rather than only genital response metrics.
