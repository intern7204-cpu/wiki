# Source Summary: Traumatic Spinal Cord Injury

## Source Type

- Textbook chapter / rehabilitation chapter.
- Authors listed in source: Paula M. Ackerman, Rebecca Hammad, Elizabeth Pharo, Jennifer Douglas, Cathi Dugger, Polly Hopkins, Lauren Nieves, and Erin A. Prentice.
- Single-source ingest only; no claims in this page are synthesized with other SCI sources.

## Reliability Level

- Evidence strength varies by subtopic: classification and care-continuum principles are high-confidence; pharmacologic neuroprotection, neuromodulation, cell-based therapy, and some technology claims are evolving.

## One-Sentence Summary

Traumatic SCI management is a continuous pathway from acute stabilization and ISNCSCI/AIS classification to secondary-complication prevention, individualized functional training, assistive technology, health promotion, discharge planning, and lifelong community reintegration.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Traumatic SCI 急性處置與復健銜接

#### One-Sentence Definition

Traumatic SCI acute care is not only spine imaging or surgery; it is airway/MAP support, selective immobilization, neurological classification, realignment/decompression, complication prevention, and early rehabilitation planning.

#### Known Facts

- In suspected cervical spine injury or SCI mechanism, trained EMS triage, immobilization, and rapid transport to a level one trauma center are recommended.
- Immobilization is not recommended for penetrating injuries when it delays resuscitation, and is also not recommended for awake, alert, nonintoxicated trauma patients without neck pain, abnormal sensory/motor changes, or significant associated injury.
- High-quality CT is the recommended initial radiologic assessment for cervical spine injury; ISNCSCI is the preferred neurologic assessment tool.
- Important acute management elements include airway, MAP maintenance, radiographic assessment, realignment, immobilization or internal stabilization, MRI when indicated, and early operative decompression delayed only for medical stabilization.
- The chapter states that steroids are not recommended as routine treatment, with consideration only within the first 8 hours after injury in appropriate instances.
- In acute SCI, an important goal during the first week is maintaining MAP around 85-90 mm Hg.
- In thoracolumbar SCI requiring surgery, consensus described in the chapter favors surgery once hemodynamically and respiratorily stable, usually within 24-48 hours.
- Inpatient rehabilitation begins during critical and acute-care stages; early therapy includes out-of-bed activity, upright tolerance, ROM, early strengthening, skin management, education, discharge planning, and care partner training.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Traumatic force
→ primary cord injury
→ secondary injury cascade / spinal shock / neurogenic shock risk
→ acute stabilization + perfusion + decompression / alignment decisions
→ early complication prevention and rehabilitation readiness
→ functional training and discharge planning
```

#### Inferences

- If acute care treats the spine but delays skin, pulmonary, bladder, mobility, and family-training planning, the patient enters rehabilitation with preventable barriers.
- MAP support, surgical stabilization, and early mobilization are not competing priorities; they are sequential conditions for safer participation.

#### Assumptions

- The patient is medically stable enough for staged rehabilitation decisions.
- ISNCSCI/AIS classification is performed correctly and repeated when spinal shock or medical instability limits interpretation.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Pediatric traumatic SCI requires specialized management and is explicitly outside this chapter's scope.
- Neuroprotection and regenerative interventions remain ongoing research territory; they should not be treated as standard functional restoration.

### Concept: ISNCSCI/AIS classification and functional prognosis

#### One-Sentence Definition

SCI severity language must be anchored in ISNCSCI/AIS, especially sacral sparing, rather than intuitive descriptions such as "no leg movement."

#### Known Facts

- Complete lesion means absent sensory and motor function in S4-S5 and corresponds to AIS A.
- Incomplete injury requires residual sensory or motor function below the neurological level, specifically including sacral sparing through anal mucocutaneous junction sensation, deep anal pressure, or voluntary anal contraction.
- Sensory testing covers 28 dermatomes bilaterally; motor testing uses 10 key muscles and a 5-point grading scale.
- Severity is determined by S4-S5 sensory testing, deep anal pressure, and voluntary anal contraction.
- SCIM III is described as a primary SCI-specific functional outcome measure and has evidence of validity and reliability.
- Anticipated functional outcomes vary by neurological level but are general guidelines, especially more applicable to complete SCI than incomplete SCI.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Segmental sensory / motor testing
→ sensory level + motor level + neurological level of injury
→ sacral sparing decision
→ AIS grade
→ prognosis, equipment needs, goal setting, discharge planning
```

#### Inferences

- Functional prognosis becomes unreliable when vertebral level, neurological level, and AIS completeness are mixed.
- In incomplete SCI, repeated functional assessment is more important than a one-time level-based prediction.

#### Assumptions

- Non-SCI conditions that affect testing are documented rather than silently folded into SCI classification.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Discomplete SCI may have neurophysiological residual brain influence below a clinically complete lesion; ISNCSCI does not directly test this dimension.

### Concept: Co-occurring brain and spinal cord injury

#### One-Sentence Definition

Co-occurring TBI changes SCI rehabilitation because cognition, motor learning, and task carryover become primary determinants of functional outcomes.

#### Known Facts

- Co-occurring TBI in patients with spinal injury is estimated at 32.5%, increasing to about 40.4% with cervical spinal injury.
- Certain risk factors such as motor vehicle collision or fall may push reported co-occurrence toward 60% or more.
- Imaging-only diagnostic approaches report lower rates than approaches that include posttraumatic amnesia.
- Co-occurring TBI is often undiagnosed or undocumented because life-saving SCI or polytrauma needs dominate early care.
- Cognitive deficits may include decreased processing speed, decreased new learning, decreased verbal fluency, inattention, executive dysfunction, and memory impairment.
- Without proper identification, patients may be mislabeled as noncompliant, poorly motivated, unable to learn, or having a bad attitude.
- Rancho level can guide errorless learning versus error-based learning: Rancho VII or less tends to require errorless learning, while Rancho VIII or greater may benefit from trial-and-error learning.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
TSCI + missed or underdocumented TBI
→ impaired attention / memory / executive function
→ reduced motor learning and carryover for transfers, bed mobility, dressing, wheelchair skills
→ apparent noncompliance or poor coping label
→ need for cognitive-informed SCI training
```

#### Inferences

- SCI rehabilitation failure in this subgroup may reflect an instructional mismatch rather than lack of effort.
- Screening for loss of consciousness and posttraumatic amnesia should be treated as functionally relevant, not merely acute trauma documentation.

#### Assumptions

- Cognitive therapy input is available or the rehab team can at least adjust environment, cueing, pacing, and practice structure.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Reported incidence varies widely depending on diagnostic method.

### Concept: Secondary complication prevention as rehabilitation foundation

#### One-Sentence Definition

In SCI, secondary complications are not side issues; they determine mortality, hospitalization, therapy tolerance, and long-term participation.

#### Known Facts

- About 70% of people living with SCI have at least one nonneurological complication or secondary condition during inpatient rehabilitation.
- High cervical injuries C1-C4 are 2.2 times more likely to have at least one complication.
- Pressure injury risk is increased during spinal shock, hard-surface positioning, backboard use, immobility, complete injury, and older age.
- Preventive skin care starts with inspection, risk assessment, bony-prominence protection, and immediate turning schedules; initial bed turns are at least every 2 hours with careful monitoring before extension.
- High cervical injuries are 3.3 times more likely to develop respiratory complications.
- Respiratory risk mechanisms include impaired diaphragm/intercostal/abdominal muscle function, weak cough, secretion retention, atelectasis, and aspiration when dysphagia is present.
- RMT can improve pulmonary function and respiratory muscle strength measures in SCI.
- UTI incidence within the first year after SCI is reported as 62%; indwelling catheter has the highest UTI risk, followed by CIC, external drainage, and volitional voiding.
- Upright mobilization must monitor orthostatic hypotension; lesions above T12 may need abdominal binder/corset support for venous return and respiratory mechanics.
- Wheelchair pressure relief is commonly maintained for 60 seconds every 30-60 minutes; forward lean and side-to-side methods may reduce upper-limb load compared with repeated push-up pressure relief.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
SCI level / completeness
→ autonomic, pulmonary, skin, bowel, bladder, musculoskeletal vulnerability
→ complications during acute and inpatient rehab
→ reduced therapy intensity and participation
→ rehospitalization / mortality / long-term functional loss
```

#### Inferences

- A plan that focuses only on walking or transfer training is incomplete if it lacks skin, pulmonary, bladder, bowel, autonomic, and cardiometabolic surveillance.
- Pressure-relief education must include real-world adherence systems such as timers, attendant instruction, and wheelchair seating match.

#### Assumptions

- Complication prevention is started before the patient reaches full inpatient rehabilitation intensity.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Some intervention details, such as ideal secretion-clearance combination or weight-bearing dose for bone health, remain uncertain.

### Concept: Functional training, wheelchair/seating, and walking goals

#### One-Sentence Definition

SCI functional training should balance realistic mobility priorities, wheelchair/seating prescription, upper-limb preservation, and walking recovery when appropriate.

#### Known Facts

- In complete high tetraplegia, verbal direction of care is itself a functional goal.
- Bed mobility and transfers often use compensatory strategies based on momentum and head-hip relationships.
- Seating should address pressure distribution, posture, comfort, quality of life, fixed deformity compensation, transportation, and functional independence.
- The chapter states that many people with SCI spend 8-12 hours or more per day in wheelchairs, making seating one of the most important interventions.
- Manual wheelchair efficiency depends on fit, rear axle position, rolling resistance, propulsion technique, and shoulder-load reduction.
- Power assist may fit users with upper-extremity weakness, joint degeneration, pain, reduced endurance, or a need to delay secondary shoulder injury.
- The chapter reports that 25%-34% of people with SCI become functional ambulators.
- A validated predictor for independent outdoor walking includes L3 and L5 strength, S1 sensation, and SCIM item 14 score of 6 or greater.
- For incomplete SCI more than 6 months post injury and already walking, the cited locomotor CPG supports high-intensity gait training that is gait-specific, aerobically challenging, and high repetition; it recommends gait training without exoskeletal robotics for that target.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Residual motor / sensory function + trunk control + UE capacity
→ feasible mobility strategy
→ equipment and training match
→ community participation and quality of life
```

#### Inferences

- Walking as a goal should not displace wheelchair skill and transfer competence when those are the fastest route to community function.
- Equipment delay can directly degrade learned function through skin breakdown, contracture, posture loss, and dependence.

#### Assumptions

- The team reassesses function and equipment fit across inpatient, home, outpatient, and community settings.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Exoskeletons may have selected uses, but this source warns against treating them as the default gait-training solution for ambulatory incomplete SCI.

### Concept: Neuroplasticity, restorative technology, and wellness

#### One-Sentence Definition

SCI recovery-oriented rehabilitation uses experience-dependent neuroplasticity principles, but technology only helps when embedded in specific, intense, salient, actively participated training.

#### Known Facts

- The chapter presents 10 principles of experience-dependent neuroplasticity, including use it or lose it, use it and improve it, specificity, repetition, intensity, time, age, salience, transference, and interference.
- Activity-based restorative therapy concepts include weight-bearing activities, FES, task-specific practice, massed practice, and locomotor training, but components should match the target function.
- Technology may increase repetition or intensity and reduce therapist burden, but technology alone does not cause useful neuroplastic change.
- Maladaptive plasticity can occur, such as increased spasticity from excitatory spinal interneuron recruitment or unwanted inhibitory/excitatory imbalance.
- SCI is associated with cardiometabolic disease risk, including CVD, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, obesity, physical deconditioning, and sedentary behavior.
- The chapter cites SCI-specific adult exercise guidelines: 20 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise twice weekly plus 3 sets of strength exercise twice weekly for major functioning muscle groups; for cardiometabolic health, 30 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise 3 times weekly.
- The Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine obesity cutoff cited in the chapter is BMI >=22 kg/m2 for people with SCI.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Specific active practice + repetition + intensity + salience
→ adaptive neuroplasticity / skill improvement
→ functional independence

Sedentary SCI physiology + reduced muscle mass + autonomic dysregulation
→ obesity / insulin resistance / CVD risk
→ need for exercise, nutrition, and behavioral support
```

#### Inferences

- Rehabilitation technology should be prescribed as a training amplifier, not as a substitute for therapist reasoning.
- Wellness counseling should begin during rehabilitation rather than being postponed until chronic follow-up.

#### Assumptions

- The patient has access to safe exercise options and barrier-aware coaching.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Regenerative, neuromodulatory, and cell-based therapies remain research-active and should be framed as evolving rather than routine standard care.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Complete SCI means absent sacral sparing at S4-S5, not simply absent leg movement.
- Spinal shock can obscure final neurological classification; repeat assessment may be necessary.
- In acute traumatic SCI, preserve airway, perfusion, alignment, and early decompression/stabilization logic while starting complication prevention immediately.
- Co-occurring TBI should be actively considered after motor vehicle collision, fall, cervical injury, loss of consciousness, or posttraumatic amnesia.
- Labeling a patient with SCI as noncompliant without assessing cognition and motor learning is unsafe.
- Pressure relief, bowel/bladder care, respiratory clearance, orthostatic management, and equipment ordering are core rehabilitation interventions, not nursing side tasks.
- Walking goals must be balanced against wheelchair skills, transfers, ADL, upper-limb preservation, and community participation.

## Research-Useful Points

- Discomplete SCI raises a gap between clinical completeness and neurophysiological residual influence below lesion level.
- Neuroplasticity-based technologies need outcome framing by active participation, specificity, repetition, intensity, and transfer, not by device novelty.
- Co-occurring TBI rates depend heavily on diagnostic definition; future work should specify whether loss of consciousness and posttraumatic amnesia were included.
- SCI cardiometabolic risk studies should avoid applying general-population BMI thresholds without SCI-specific interpretation.

## Conflicts With Existing Knowledge

- "Steroids for acute SCI" should not be written as routine standard care. This chapter states steroids are not recommended routinely, and only considered within the first 8 hours in selected cases.
- Exoskeletons and robotic systems should not be treated as default superior gait training. For incomplete SCI more than 6 months post injury and already walking, the cited CPG favors high-intensity gait training and recommends gait training without exoskeletal robotics.
- "Rehab starts after acute stabilization" is too late. This source frames rehabilitation as beginning during acute and critical-care stages.
- "Walking is the main success metric" is too narrow. The source repeatedly ties outcome to functional independence, ADL, seating, equipment, community participation, and quality of life.
