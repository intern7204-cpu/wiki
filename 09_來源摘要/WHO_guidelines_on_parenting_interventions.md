# Source Summary: WHO guidelines on parenting interventions

## Source Type

- WHO guideline.
- Full title: *WHO guidelines on parenting interventions to prevent maltreatment and enhance parent-child relationships with children aged 0-17 years*.
- Publication year: 2022.
- This summary uses only the guideline file at `C:\原始資料\WHO guidelines on parenting interventions\WHO guidelines on parenting interventions.md`; the linked Web Annex and underlying review reports are not separately ingested here.

## Reliability Level

- Tier 1 for policy-level recommendations on evidence-based parenting interventions.
- Methodology: WHO guideline development process, GRADE evidence assessment, four systematic reviews, one narrative review, and WHO-INTEGRATE considerations.
- Strongest for defining who should have access to parenting interventions, what broad outcomes are supported, and what implementation safeguards matter.
- Less certain for adolescent-specific, humanitarian-setting, indicated maltreatment-response, long-term, cost-effectiveness, and male caregiver questions because the guideline itself marks these evidence areas as limited.

## One-Sentence Summary

WHO recommends making evidence-based parenting interventions accessible to parents and caregivers of children aged 0-17 years to reduce child maltreatment and harsh parenting, improve parent-child relationships, and support child and parent mental health, while preserving program fidelity and integrating child protection when harm is serious.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Evidence-based parenting interventions for maltreatment prevention

#### One-Sentence Definition

Evidence-based parenting interventions are structured, evaluated programs that teach caregivers practical parenting skills and relationship practices to reduce child maltreatment / harsh parenting and improve parent-child interaction.

#### Known Facts

- The guideline addresses parents and caregivers of children aged 0-17 years.
- Target outcomes include child maltreatment, harsh and negative parenting, positive parenting skills and behaviours, child externalizing / internalizing problems, parental mental health, and parenting stress.
- WHO makes five strong recommendations:
  - LMIC parents / caregivers of children aged 2-17 years should have ready access to evidence-based parenting interventions.
  - Globally, parents / caregivers of children aged 2-10 years should have ready access to social learning theory-informed parenting interventions.
  - In LMICs, parents / caregivers of adolescents aged 10-17 years should have access to adolescent-sensitive parenting interventions.
  - In humanitarian settings within LMICs, parents / caregivers of children aged 0-17 years should have access to parenting interventions or broader interventions with parenting components, with attention to recipient mental health.
  - For children aged 0-3 years, the guideline reiterates responsive caregiving, early learning, nutrition-linked caregiving support, and maternal mental health integration from the WHO ECD guideline.
- Evidence certainty varies by population and outcome:
  - For ages 2-10 globally, evidence is moderate for maltreatment / harsh parenting, positive parenting, externalizing behaviour, and parental mental health; low for internalizing behaviour and parenting stress.
  - For adolescents in LMICs, positive parenting evidence is low, and several maltreatment / behaviour / parent mental health outcomes are very low certainty.
  - For humanitarian settings, positive parenting evidence is moderate, harsh parenting / internalizing / parent mental health are low, and maltreatment / externalizing / parenting stress are very low.
- Most evidence-based programs include combinations of nonviolent discipline, positive reinforcement, proactive parenting, parental self-management, child-led play, empathy building, and skill practice.
- The guideline states that skill-building and practice matter more than information or attitude change alone.
- Adding more parenting components does not automatically increase effectiveness.
- Time-out has evidence as a component for reducing conduct problems, but proper implementation and caution are required.
- Parenting interventions can be group-based or individualized, delivered by professional or paraprofessional staff, and embedded in health, education, social welfare, community, NGO, home, centre, or digital / hybrid settings.
- Where serious maltreatment or severe parent-child conflict is occurring, specialized parenting interventions plus child protection service interventions should be considered.
- Parenting support should complement, not substitute for, poverty reduction, public health, and broader violence-prevention strategies.
- Cultural adaptation is acceptable when it preserves core components and fidelity; risky adaptation includes cutting sessions, removing key messages or skills, changing the theoretical approach, or using inadequately trained staff.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Structured caregiver skill practice
-> more nonviolent discipline, positive reinforcement, monitoring, empathy, and relationship repair
-> less harsh / negative parenting and better parent-child interaction
-> lower child maltreatment risk and fewer child behavioural / emotional problems
-> better family functioning, with effect size and certainty depending on age group, context, and implementation quality
```

#### Inferences

- A parenting intervention should be treated as a program with mechanism, dosage, fidelity, workforce, and monitoring requirements, not as a single counseling phrase.
- Clinical referral should distinguish universal, selective, and indicated prevention; indicated or active maltreatment situations need a higher-skill and child-protection-aware response.
- In humanitarian or high-stress contexts, parenting support must be paired with trauma-informed psychosocial care and attention to caregiver mental health when indicated.
- For 0-3 years, responsive caregiving and early learning are plausible maltreatment-risk reducers, but the source says direct child maltreatment outcomes were limited in the ECD evidence base.

#### Assumptions

- Programs chosen locally have outcome evidence or a clear evidence-based logic model.
- Staff can be trained, supervised, and supported enough to preserve fidelity.
- Families have practical access, including scheduling, transport, language, cultural fit, and nonjudgmental delivery.
- Acute safety risks are assessed and routed through child protection pathways rather than left to parenting education alone.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Long-term effects beyond 6-12 months remain insufficiently studied in several populations.
- Evidence is thin for adolescents in LMICs, families already identified as maltreating, humanitarian settings beyond conflict / displacement, extreme poverty, male caregivers, and cost-effectiveness.
- Many outcomes are parent-reported; independent observation and child / adolescent self-report are underused.
- Population-level effects are unlikely unless programs reach large proportions of a community or explicitly target community parenting norms.
- The guideline was published in 2022 and planned review around five years or earlier if major new evidence emerges.

## Clinically Useful Points

- Do not equate parenting intervention with vague advice. A useful plan names the program type, target behaviours, caregiver practice method, access barriers, and follow-up.
- For child behaviour problems, parent-child relationship strain, harsh discipline, or maltreatment risk, assess caregiver stress, safety, cultural fit, parent mental health, transport / cost barriers, and child protection needs.
- Serious maltreatment is not a routine parent-training-only situation; it requires specialized intervention and child protection service involvement.
- In infants and toddlers, responsive care, early learning activities, nutrition-linked caregiving support, and maternal mental health support are part of the relevant prevention frame.
- Group delivery can provide peer problem-solving; individualized delivery can provide closer tailoring.

## Research-Useful Points

- Priority research gaps include adolescent interventions in LMICs, interventions for parents who have already maltreated children, humanitarian settings, extreme poverty, male caregivers, long-term outcomes, independent observational outcomes, child / adolescent self-report, IPV outcomes, sociocultural acceptability, equity effects, cost-effectiveness, and lay workforce delivery.
