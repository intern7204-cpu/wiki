## One-Sentence Summary

Brainstem syndromes are best approached by objective physical findings: cranial nerve level, ocular motor pattern, long tract involvement, crossed signs, consciousness, and posterior circulation context localize disease more reliably than memorizing named eponyms.

## Core Concepts Extracted

### Concept: Brainstem localization by objective signs

#### One-Sentence Definition

Brainstem localization uses the rostrocaudal distribution of cranial nerve nuclei / fascicles plus long tract signs to infer lesion level and side.

#### Known Facts

- Long tracts traverse the brainstem rostrocaudally.
- Cranial nerve nuclei and exiting cranial nerves occupy distinct brainstem levels.
- Crossed deficits are characteristic: ipsilateral cranial nerve signs with contralateral long tract motor or sensory findings.
- Coma, ataxia, and vertigo are common in vertebrobasilar posterior circulation lesions and less common in carotid anterior circulation syndromes.
- INO, unreactive pupils, lower motor neuron cranial nerve impairment, and skew deviation from stroke occur only with posterior circulation lesions in the source.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Focal brainstem lesion
-> cranial nerve nucleus / fascicle sign at lesion level
-> descending / ascending tract interruption
-> ipsilateral cranial nerve deficit + contralateral long tract deficit
-> brainstem localization
```

#### Inferences

- If a syndrome lacks objective brainstem signs, a peripheral cranial nerve, neuromuscular junction, muscle, or functional mimic should be considered.
- Multiple patchy posterior circulation lesions may not fit one neat transverse brainstem syndrome.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- The source emphasizes diagnosis by signs but does not replace MRI / vascular imaging in acute stroke.

### Concept: Brainstem ocular motor syndromes

#### One-Sentence Definition

Ocular motor patterns such as vertical gaze palsy, dorsal midbrain syndrome, INO, horizontal gaze paresis, and one-and-a-half syndrome provide high-yield localization clues.

#### Known Facts

- Combined vertical gaze ophthalmoplegia involves impaired upward and downward gaze and localizes to riMLF pathways.
- Dorsal midbrain / Parinaud syndrome classically includes upgaze palsy, light-near dissociation, convergence-retraction nystagmus, and lid retraction.
- INO results from MLF lesion and produces impaired adduction on horizontal gaze with characteristic internuclear pattern.
- Intact convergence supports isolated MLF lesion over CN III / medial rectus / neuromuscular junction disorders.
- One-and-a-half syndrome reflects a PPRF or CN VI nucleus lesion plus internuclear fibers.
- Global ophthalmoplegia in isolation is usually extraaxial, such as cranial nerve, neuromuscular junction, or muscle disease.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Discrete gaze network lesion
-> selective saccade / pursuit / VOR / convergence pattern
-> ocular motor syndrome
-> anatomic localization and differential diagnosis
```

#### Inferences

- Acute isolated vertical gaze palsy in an older adult should raise concern for infarction, while gradual progressive downgaze problems in older adults raise PSP in the source.
- MG, Miller Fisher syndrome, GBS, thyroid eye disease, chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia, and cavernous sinus disease can mimic brainstem ocular syndromes.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- Ocular motor signs may be difficult to elicit when mental status, cooperation, or reticular formation function is impaired.

### Concept: Brainstem stroke, crossed signs, and locked-in syndrome

#### One-Sentence Definition

Brainstem ischemic stroke often presents with long tract involvement plus cranial nerve deficits; bilateral ventral pontine involvement can cause locked-in syndrome with preserved consciousness.

#### Known Facts

- Vertebrobasilar lesions may be rostrocaudal and patchy rather than simple transverse syndromes.
- Bilateral long tract involvement can produce locked-in syndrome, classically from basis pontis lesion.
- Locked-in syndrome includes quadriplegia, corticobulbar impairment, loss of speech, preserved reticular activating system, and preserved consciousness.
- Eye movements or blinking may be the only voluntary control in locked-in syndrome.
- Pseudobulbar palsy / supranuclear bulbar palsy can arise from bilateral long tract lesions and cause dysarthria, dysphagia, bilateral facial weakness, extremity weakness, and emotional lability.
- A prospective series cited in the source found that only a minority of brainstem infarcts fit classic named eponymic syndromes.

#### Mechanism Chain

```text
Posterior circulation ischemia
-> brainstem tract and cranial nerve involvement
-> crossed or bilateral motor / sensory / bulbar signs
-> possible locked-in or pseudobulbar syndrome depending on tract distribution
```

#### Inferences

- Communication assessment is urgent in locked-in syndrome because consciousness may be preserved despite near-complete motor output loss.
- Dysphagia and respiratory risk should be anticipated when bilateral corticobulbar or medullary / pontine structures are involved.

#### Uncertainties / Limitations

- This source localizes syndromes but does not define acute thrombolysis / thrombectomy indications.

## Clinical Implications

- Start with ocular motor findings, pupils, cranial nerve lower motor neuron signs, long tract motor / sensory signs, ataxia, consciousness, vertigo, dysarthria, and dysphagia.
- Do not force a named eponym when findings are partial, patchy, bilateral, or multi-level.
- In suspected posterior circulation stroke, normal anterior circulation assumptions may fail; altered mental status, vertigo, ocular motor signs, and crossed signs are high-yield.
- Locked-in syndrome requires deliberate yes / no communication testing and avoidance of mislabeling preserved consciousness as coma.

## Fact

- Dorsal midbrain syndrome can arise from pineal tumor, stroke, trauma, MS, AVM, PSP, and other causes depending on age and time course in the source.
- INO is commonly associated with stroke in older adults and MS in younger adults in the source.
- Horizontal gaze paresis can result from frontopontine, mesencephalic reticular formation, PPRF, or CN VI nucleus lesions.
- Syringobulbia is a rostrocaudal cavity involving medulla and spinal cord, and MRI is described as reliable and sensitive.

## Inference

- A localization-first approach reduces false confidence from memorized syndromes.
- Eye movement examination can separate central brainstem lesions from extraaxial mimics when convergence, VOR, pupil, ptosis, and fatigability are tested.

## Assumption

- The neurologic examination is reliable enough to interpret, or limitations are documented.

## Uncertainty

- Some clinical signs may be false localizing signs due to brainstem compression or systemic mimics.
- Posterior circulation stroke diagnosis still requires imaging and vascular evaluation.
