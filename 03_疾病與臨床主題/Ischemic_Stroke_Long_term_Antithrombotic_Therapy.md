# Ischemic Stroke Long-term Antithrombotic Therapy

## One-Sentence Definition

Ischemic stroke long-term antithrombotic therapy is the mechanism-based selection of single antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, time-limited DAPT, or selected low-dose anticoagulant plus antiplatelet therapy after the acute stroke/TIA window, with noncardioembolic stroke/TIA defaulting to single antiplatelet therapy rather than indefinite DAPT.

## Definition and Boundary

This page covers long-term antithrombotic selection after ischemic stroke or TIA. It does not cover:

- acute thrombolysis / thrombectomy
- first-days antithrombotic decisions
- detailed AF anticoagulation timing and anticoagulation failure workflow；見 [[AF_Stroke_Anticoagulation_時機與長期策略]] 與 [[AF_Stroke_Anticoagulation_Failure]]
- full coronary stent DAPT management
- primary prevention

The central boundary is phase and mechanism:

- Acute minor stroke / high-risk TIA may justify short-term DAPT in selected patients.
- Long-term noncardioembolic stroke / TIA usually returns to single antiplatelet therapy.
- Cardioembolic mechanisms such as AF or LV thrombus belong to anticoagulation pathways.
- Cryptogenic stroke / ESUS is generally treated with antiplatelet therapy unless a true anticoagulation indication is found.

## Why It Matters

Antithrombotic errors after stroke are common because different decisions are collapsed into one phrase: "blood thinner." That imprecision causes three opposite errors:

- undertreatment: AF or LV thrombus treated like noncardioembolic stroke
- overtreatment: long-term DAPT without an indication
- mistargeting: breakthrough stroke labeled as antiplatelet failure before mechanism and adherence are reassessed

## Preconditions or Conditions

Before choosing long-term therapy, the chart should answer:

- Is the index event ischemic stroke or TIA?
- Is the likely mechanism noncardioembolic, cardioembolic, lacunar, cryptogenic/ESUS, dissection, hypercoagulable, or procedure-related?
- Is the patient still in an acute short-term DAPT window?
- Is there a separate indication for anticoagulation, such as AF or LV thrombus?
- Is there a separate indication for DAPT, such as recent coronary stent, ACS, or carotid stent?
- Is bleeding risk, medication allergy, cost, access, adherence, and GI risk documented?

## Mechanism

### Noncardioembolic recurrence

Arterial plaque or small-vessel disease -> platelet activation and aggregation -> platelet-rich thrombus -> recurrent ischemic stroke/TIA.  
Therefore, long-term single antiplatelet therapy is the default.

### Cardioembolic recurrence

AF or LV thrombus -> coagulation-driven intracardiac thrombus -> embolization to cerebral arteries.  
Therefore, anticoagulation is the core treatment when bleeding risk and timing permit.

### Why long-term DAPT usually fails as default

Dual platelet inhibition can reduce early recurrent events in selected acute settings, but the long-term bleeding penalty accumulates. In most noncardioembolic stroke/TIA patients, aspirin plus clopidogrel does not add enough long-term efficacy to justify the bleeding risk.

## Clinical / Research Implication

### Clinical implication

For a post-stroke medication review, ask: "Does this antithrombotic choice match the documented mechanism and phase?" This is more precise than asking whether the patient is taking an antiplatelet or anticoagulant.

Practical checks:

- DAPT has a stop date unless a separate stent/ACS indication exists.
- AF, LV thrombus, mechanical valve, or APS are not treated as ordinary noncardioembolic stroke.
- Cryptogenic stroke triggers more mechanism search, not automatic anticoagulation.
- Breakthrough stroke triggers adherence and mechanism reassessment before resistance testing.

### Research implication

Three areas remain active:

- Which prior-stroke patients have enough systemic atherosclerotic risk to justify aspirin plus low-dose rivaroxaban?
- Whether Factor XIa inhibitors will become a safer add-on strategy for noncardioembolic stroke prevention.
- Whether pharmacogenomic or platelet-function testing can be tied to outcome-improving treatment changes rather than only laboratory response.

## Fact

- Aspirin, clopidogrel, and aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole are acceptable long-term options for recurrent noncardioembolic ischemic stroke prevention.
- For noncardioembolic stroke/TIA of atherothrombotic, lacunar, or cryptogenic type, the source recommends antiplatelet therapy using one of those agents.
- The source suggests clopidogrel monotherapy or aspirin-extended-release dipyridamole rather than aspirin alone when tolerated and feasible.
- Cilostazol is reasonable for patients of East Asian ethnicity and when other agents are unavailable or not tolerated.
- Long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel is not recommended for most noncardioembolic stroke/TIA patients.
- Carotid stenting commonly uses aspirin plus clopidogrel for about 30 days, then long-term single antiplatelet therapy.
- Cryptogenic stroke / ESUS is generally not an anticoagulation indication.
- Routine aspirin or clopidogrel resistance testing is not proven as a standard response to antiplatelet treatment failure.
- For most ischemic stroke / TIA patients with AF, the AF stroke source recommends DOAC rather than VKA for long-term anticoagulation.
- The AF stroke source states VKA remains indicated in mechanical valves and clinically significant rheumatic mitral stenosis.
- The AF stroke source states breakthrough stroke on anticoagulation requires evaluation of cardioembolic and noncardioembolic mechanisms, including adherence and anticoagulant intensity.
- The ICAS treatment source suggests DAPT with aspirin plus clopidogrel up to 90 days for recent stroke / TIA attributed to 70-99% atherosclerotic intracranial stenosis.
- The ICAS treatment source uses aspirin monotherapy for long-term prevention after the 21-day or 90-day DAPT window.
- The ICAS treatment source states warfarin is harmful for TIA or stroke due to ICAS and has no routine role for ICAS alone.

## Inference

- Indefinite DAPT after ischemic stroke should be treated as a problem to explain, not as a default to continue.
- If a recurrent event occurs while the patient is taking antiplatelet therapy, wrong mechanism and nonadherence are often more actionable than presumed pharmacologic resistance.
- In a rehabilitation setting, medication reconciliation should include planned DAPT stop date, mechanism label, and whether anticoagulation has been considered for cardioembolic sources.
- For AF patients, "anticoagulated" is not enough information; adequacy of dose, adherence, renal adjustment and interactions determine whether the mechanism was actually treated.
- ICAS should be treated as a mechanism-specific DAPT exception: the duration is tied to stenosis severity and qualifying event risk, not to a generic "noncardioembolic stroke" label alone.

## Assumption

- The patient has no active major bleeding or absolute contraindication to antithrombotic therapy.
- Stroke mechanism has been evaluated well enough to place the patient into noncardioembolic versus cardioembolic pathways.
- Doses and timing are adjusted for renal function, drug interactions, procedures, hemorrhagic transformation risk, and local specialist guidance.

## Uncertainty

- Optimal strategy after true breakthrough noncardioembolic stroke on adherent antiplatelet therapy remains uncertain.
- Cilostazol evidence may not generalize from East Asian populations to other populations.
- The role of Factor XIa inhibitors is not yet settled as routine care.
- Aspirin plus low-dose rivaroxaban is promising for selected atherosclerotic patients, but not a universal post-stroke regimen.

## Limitations and Misreadings

- Misreading 1: "Short-term DAPT works, so long-term DAPT is stronger prevention."  
  Correction: the source separates acute short-term DAPT from long-term prevention and recommends against routine long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel.

- Misreading 2: "Cryptogenic means embolic, so anticoagulate."  
  Correction: cryptogenic stroke / ESUS is generally treated with antiplatelet therapy unless a specific anticoagulation indication is discovered.

- Misreading 3: "CYP2C19 matters, so every stroke patient on clopidogrel needs genotype testing."  
  Correction: pharmacologic plausibility exists, but the source does not support routine testing as a proven outcome-improving workflow.

- Misreading 4: "Aspirin plus rivaroxaban replaces antiplatelet therapy for all stroke patients."  
  Correction: it is only a selected option for nonlacunar, noncardioembolic patients with systemic atherosclerosis and acceptable bleeding risk.

- Misreading 5: "Stroke on anticoagulation means add antiplatelet."  
  Correction: the AF stroke source prioritizes exposure and mechanism reassessment; routine antiplatelet addition after warfarin breakthrough has higher hemorrhage concern and less defined benefit than optimizing anticoagulation.

- Misreading 6: "ICAS severe stenosis means long-term dual antiplatelet therapy."  
  Correction: the ICAS source supports time-limited DAPT for selected recent symptomatic stenosis, then single antiplatelet therapy.
